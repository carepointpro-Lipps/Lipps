LIPPS LEARN · HEALTH COMMUNICATION · WEEK 11

Communication Campaign Design and Evaluation

If the objective cannot be measured, the campaign cannot tell whether it achieved anything beyond activity.

Campaign planning Theory of change Indicators Evaluation

This week in one sentence

Design a coherent health communication campaign from problem diagnosis through objectives, strategy, implementation, monitoring and evaluation.

The LIPPS lens
Good health communication asks not only whether a message was delivered, but who interpreted it, under what conditions, what action became possible, and what evidence supports the claimed effect.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. distinguish health, behavioural and communication problems;
  2. write specific measurable communication objectives;
  3. develop a communication theory of change;
  4. align audience, mechanism, message, messenger and channel;
  5. distinguish inputs, activities, outputs, reach, intermediate outcomes, behaviour and health outcomes;
  6. select process and outcome indicators;
  7. explain implementation fidelity and dose/exposure;
  8. choose feasible evaluation designs and recognise attribution limits;
  9. combine quantitative and qualitative evaluation evidence;
  10. assess equity, cost and unintended effects.

ORIENT · A successful campaign because 100,000 flyers were printed?

A programme reports that its maternal-health campaign was successful because it produced 100,000 flyers, aired 300 radio spots and reached 1.2 million social-media impressions. The original objective was to increase early antenatal booking. No measure of awareness, comprehension, intention, service enquiries or booking was collected.

Activities and reach are useful implementation data. They are not automatically evidence that the campaign changed the intended outcome.

LEARN 1 · Start with the problem hierarchy

Separate the health problem from the behavioural or service problem and from the communication problem. For example: maternal complications are the health problem; late antenatal initiation may be a behaviour/service problem; misunderstanding when ANC should begin may be one communication determinant.

LEARN 2 · Write measurable objectives

An objective should specify who, what change, how much, and by when where feasible. “Raise awareness” is weak unless awareness is defined. “Increase from 45% to 65% the proportion of first-time pregnant women who know ANC should begin in the first trimester within six months” is clearer.

LEARN 3 · Build a communication theory of change

Connect determinant → communication mechanism → exposure/experience → intermediate change → behaviour → health contribution. Include assumptions about service availability and social context. A theory of change makes it possible to identify where failure occurred.

LEARN 4 · Align strategy components

The audience, objective, message, messenger and channel should fit one another. A complex decision requiring privacy and dialogue is poorly matched to a billboard. A reminder problem may be well suited to SMS if phones are private and numbers are stable.

LEARN 5 · Use an outcome ladder

Inputs are resources. Activities are what the programme does. Outputs are products delivered. Reach/exposure captures contact with communication. Intermediate outcomes include knowledge, beliefs, skills, norms or intentions. Behaviour/service outcomes reflect action. Health outcomes are usually influenced by many additional factors.

LEARN 6 · Process evaluation explains implementation

Measure whether communication was delivered as planned, with adequate quality, frequency and coverage. Fidelity asks whether the intervention matched its intended design. Dose concerns amount delivered or received. Process data help distinguish strategy failure from implementation failure.

LEARN 7 · Outcome evaluation needs a counterfactual idea

A simple before-after change may reflect secular trends, other programmes, seasonality or changing populations. Stronger designs may use comparison groups, interrupted time series, repeated cross-sectional surveys or experimental allocation where ethical and feasible. Communication evaluation should borrow rigor from epidemiology.

LEARN 8 · Attribution becomes harder further down the chain

It is usually easier to attribute a change in message comprehension to a campaign than a decline in mortality. Health outcomes depend on service quality, access, policy, biology and other exposures. Be explicit about contribution versus attribution.

LEARN 9 · Qualitative evidence explains mechanisms and unintended effects

Interviews, observation, feedback logs and focus groups can reveal why a message was trusted, why a channel excluded some groups, how norms shifted or why a seemingly successful campaign produced stigma.

LEARN 10 · Evaluate equity and cost

Ask who was reached, who benefited, who was missed and what it cost. A campaign with strong average effects can still widen disparities if already advantaged groups receive most of the benefit.

DEEPEN · A campaign is a managed intervention, not a collection of products

Campaign planning should connect the health problem to a communication problem, a defined audience, measurable objectives, a strategy, implementation activities and an evaluation plan. The NCI Making Health Communication Programs Work model emphasises planning, developing and testing concepts and materials, implementation, and assessment.

Start with a communication diagnosis

Specify the behaviour or decision, then separate communication determinants from service and structural determinants. If contraceptive uptake is limited because stock-outs are frequent, a campaign that increases demand without addressing supply may worsen trust.

Objectives form a ladder

Distinguish outputs from outcomes. “Broadcast 100 radio spots” is an activity. “Increase correct knowledge of where free screening is available from 45% to 70%” is a communication objective. “Reduce cervical cancer mortality” is an important health goal but too distal to use as the sole communication indicator.

Strategy before products

A strategy states who must do what, what currently prevents that action, what value proposition or mechanism will be used, who should communicate, through which channels and when. Posters, videos and radio spots are products that implement the strategy.

Implementation planning

Specify responsibilities, production timelines, approvals, partner coordination, media placement, training, community mobilisation, supply dependencies and contingency plans. A strategy that assumes commodities or services that are unavailable is internally inconsistent.

Monitoring and evaluation

Use a results chain:

inputs → activities → outputs → reach/exposure → comprehension/trust → intention/decision → behaviour/service use → health outcome.

Different study designs support different claims. A before-and-after increase may be encouraging but does not automatically prove campaign causation if services, prices, policies or secular trends changed at the same time.

Adaptive management

Monitoring should trigger decisions. If reach is high but comprehension is low, revise the message. If comprehension is high but uptake is low, investigate trust, feasibility and service constraints rather than simply buying more airtime.

What could fool us?

Programme dashboards often reward easily counted outputs. Teams may therefore produce more materials even when the bottleneck is elsewhere. Campaign evaluation should pre-specify the most important indicators and the decisions each indicator will inform.

FIELD LAB · Build a campaign logic model

Consider a campaign to increase postpartum family-planning counselling.

Problem: many women leave maternity services without understanding available methods.

Communication objective: increase the proportion of postpartum women who can name at least two suitable options and know where to obtain counselling.

Strategy: train maternity staff in brief counselling, provide a simple decision aid, and reinforce referral information through discharge materials and SMS where safe.

Results chain:

trained staff + decision aid → counselling delivered → women exposed → understanding and confidence → counselling attendance or method choice → improved contraceptive access.

Each arrow contains an assumption. Staff may be trained but too busy to counsel. Women may understand but face partner opposition or stock-outs. Evaluation should therefore measure multiple stages rather than jumping from training delivered to contraceptive uptake.

Minimum campaign dashboard

Include one indicator for implementation quality, one for reach, one for comprehension/trust, one for intended behaviour and one for service capacity. Pre-specify what action the team will take if each indicator crosses a threshold.

Evaluation choice

If districts launch at different times, a staggered quasi-experimental analysis may be possible. If randomisation is feasible and ethical, cluster randomisation could support stronger causal inference. If only descriptive monitoring is available, state that limitation rather than converting correlation into impact.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

Design a six-month campaign to increase timely childhood vaccination after missed-dose rates rise in three districts. Routine data suggest missed appointments, but formative interviews identify transport barriers, uncertainty about catch-up eligibility, occasional stock-outs and rumours about side effects.

  1. Separate health, service/behaviour and communication problems.
  2. Write two measurable communication objectives.
  3. Build a short theory of change.
  4. Define audiences and channel mix.
  5. List inputs, activities, outputs, reach indicators, intermediate outcomes and behaviour/service outcomes.
  6. Choose an evaluation design and identify key threats to inference.
  7. Add qualitative evaluation questions.
  8. Add equity and cost indicators.
  9. State what result would lead you to change the campaign rather than continue it.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. Why are outputs not outcomes?
  2. What does implementation fidelity mean?
  3. Why is a before-after comparison vulnerable to alternative explanations?
  4. What is the difference between contribution and attribution?
  5. Give one intermediate communication outcome between exposure and behaviour.
  6. How can a campaign improve the average outcome while worsening equity?

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.

Assessment principle
Knowing the material should be the fastest strategy. Timing is calibrated to reward prepared reading and reasoning, not to replace sound question design or approved accessibility accommodations.
