# LIPPS Learn — University-readiness QA

**Version audited:** v1.7  
**Scope:** Epidemiology Weeks 1–12; Health Communication Weeks 1–12; public Quarto lessons; Moodle GIFT banks; course and assessment pages.

## Executive judgement

The LIPPS Learn architecture is strong enough for a **university pilot**, but the two courses are **not yet ready to be sold as validated, stand-alone high-stakes courseware**.

The strongest elements are the course sequence, applied public-health framing, clear weekly learning cycle, causal/interpretive restraint in Epidemiology, and the unusually strong treatment of risk communication, community engagement, infodemics, ethics and equity in Health Communication.

The main weakness is assessment validity. The existing Moodle banks are large, but item count should not be mistaken for assessment quality. Many Health Communication items are three paraphrased recognition questions built around the same definition; many Epidemiology items are repeated numerical variants of the same calculation template. These are useful **practice generators**, but a university-facing mastery bank must contain a much larger proportion of genuinely new scenarios, interpretation problems and decision questions.

A second weakness is constructive alignment. Weekly objective quizzes alone cannot adequately assess skills such as audience analysis, counselling, message design, community engagement, campaign planning, outbreak reasoning or critical appraisal. Both courses need authentic assessed products alongside quizzes.

## Epidemiology — what is working

1. The sequence from epidemiological thinking → frequency → association → description → study design → bias/causal reasoning → diagnostic testing → surveillance/outbreak → critical appraisal is coherent.
2. Weeks 1–3 are particularly rich and explanatory.
3. The course distinguishes what the attached CDC source supports from later LIPPS extensions.
4. Week 9 correctly separates confounding, mediation, collider bias and effect modification.
5. Week 10 avoids the common error of treating sensitivity, specificity and predictive value as interchangeable.
6. Week 12 gives the course an appropriate interpretive endpoint rather than ending with formula recall.

## Epidemiology — revisions needed

1. **Assessment repetition:** large blocks of parameterised items test the same operation repeatedly. Keep some as practice, but mastery banks should sample different reasoning steps.
2. **Infectious-disease foundations:** natural history, agent–host–environment reasoning and chain-of-infection concepts are underrepresented for a broad introductory epidemiology course. Add them as a concise supplement or embed them in Week 11.
3. **Standardisation:** age adjustment is introduced but not practised deeply. A short applied exercise would improve population-comparison competence.
4. **Sampling:** Week 5 introduces sampling logic, but probability sampling, cluster effects and weighting should link more explicitly to the Methods library.
5. **Authentic outputs:** add a short study-design critique, outbreak-analysis exercise and final critical-appraisal memo.
6. **Assessment timing:** current timings must remain pilot values until actual response-time data are available.

## Health Communication — what is working

1. The course does not reduce communication to posters and media products; it treats communication as a decision and implementation process.
2. Behavioural theory is linked to diagnosis rather than added decoratively after campaign design.
3. Health literacy, culture, interpersonal communication and community engagement appear before channels and media strategy.
4. Risk/crisis communication and infodemic management are given their own substantial weeks.
5. Ethics, equity, privacy, AI and manipulation are integrated rather than treated as footnotes.
6. The LIPPS SIGNAL framework creates a useful capstone planning structure.

## Health Communication — revisions needed

1. **Depth:** most weeks are around 1,000–1,300 words. For stand-alone digital courseware, add deeper explanations, comparative tables, worked communication products and assigned readings.
2. **Question bank redesign:** the largest current weakness. Many items repeat the same definition in three forms (definition → context label → term recognition). These should be reclassified as practice flash questions, not mastery assessment.
3. **Performance assessment:** health communication competence cannot be established using MCQs alone. Students should design, pre-test, revise and evaluate actual communication products.
4. **Theory week overload:** Week 2 covers HBM, TPB, Social Cognitive Theory, social-ecological thinking, COM-B and diffusion. Retain the overview, but add a comparison matrix and make students justify theory selection in a case.
5. **Clinical communication:** Week 4 needs an assessed teach-back or counselling transcript/role-play task.
6. **Community engagement:** Week 5 should assess stakeholder mapping, listening and feedback-loop design.
7. **Media strategy:** Week 7 should include a channel-mix budget or reach/frequency trade-off exercise.
8. **Digital communication:** Week 8 should distinguish platform engagement metrics from meaningful communication and health outcomes using a worked dashboard example.
9. **Referencing:** add a stable core-reading page and week-specific readings.

## Benchmark check

The Health Communication sequence is broadly consistent with current public-health communication practice. WHO's communication framework emphasizes communications that are accessible, actionable, credible, relevant, timely and understandable. WHO's 2024 RCCE competency framework emphasizes professional behavioural competencies for community-centred emergency programmes. CDC's Clear Communication Index provides a research-based tool for assessing public communication materials, while the current CDC CERC manual remains a practical emergency-communication reference. These should be treated as core benchmarks, not as the only evidence base.

The attached CDC *Principles of Epidemiology in Public Health Practice, Third Edition* remains a strong foundational source for the Epidemiology course, but it is an older introductory text. LIPPS should retain it as a foundation while clearly identifying modern extensions and updating applied examples and methodological guidance.

## Required assessment redesign before institutional launch

### Epidemiology

- **15% — weekly mastery quizzes:** objective, randomised, mostly interpretation/application.
- **20% — applied measurement exercises:** frequency, association, diagnostic testing and descriptive epidemiology.
- **20% — study-design critique:** critique one published or simulated epidemiological study.
- **20% — field/outbreak analysis:** line list, descriptive output, hypothesis and investigation plan.
- **25% — final critical-appraisal memo:** interpret design, effect estimates, bias, precision, applicability and claim strength.

### Health Communication

- **15% — weekly mastery quizzes:** objective checks, but no longer dominated by term recognition.
- **20% — audience and behaviour diagnosis brief:** define audience, determinant and theory-informed response.
- **20% — message design + pre-test assignment:** draft, pre-test evidence, revision rationale.
- **15% — risk-communication simulation:** first announcement, uncertainty statement, action message and update.
- **30% — campaign capstone:** strategy, channel mix, community feedback, implementation indicators, outcome evaluation and ethics/equity review using LIPPS SIGNAL.

These weights are a proposed pilot blueprint and should be adapted to institutional regulations.

## Assessment-bank disposition

Do **not** delete the current banks. Reclassify them:

- **Foundation/practice pool:** definition checks and repeated calculations.
- **Mastery pool:** genuine scenarios, interpretation, comparison and decision-making.
- **Institutional assessment pool:** separately reviewed, exposure-controlled items with documented blueprint coverage.

A sensible target for the next revision is **25–35 high-quality mastery items per week**, not 50 superficially different items. Smaller, better banks are preferable to large templated banks.

## Minimum institutional launch gate

LIPPS should not describe either course as high-stakes-ready until all of the following are complete:

- second subject-matter-expert review;
- explicit course assessment blueprint;
- authentic assignments and rubrics;
- mastery-bank rewrite;
- accessibility review;
- native Quarto render and browser/mobile testing;
- Moodle import test on the actual hosted instance;
- student pilot;
- item analysis after pilot;
- revision based on completion, difficulty, discrimination and response-time data; and
- local curriculum/credit-hour mapping by the adopting institution.

## Overall decision

**Proceed to pilot. Do not market the current version as a finished high-stakes assessment product.**

The curriculum is strong enough to justify a real university pilot. The next build priority is not more content categories; it is **assessment quality, authentic student work, references/readings and pilot evidence**.
