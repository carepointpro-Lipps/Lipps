LIPPS LEARN · EPIDEMIOLOGY · WEEK 10

Screening and Diagnostic-Test Evaluation

A test result changes probability; it does not create certainty.

Intermediate Screening Sensitivity · Specificity Predictive values

This week in one sentence

Evaluate screening and diagnostic classification using sensitivity, specificity and predictive values, then extend interpretation to likelihood ratios, thresholds and multi-test strategies.

The LIPPS lens
Evidence becomes useful when you can explain not only what the analysis shows, but also what the design, measurement and comparison allow you to claim.

Learning outcomes

By the end of Week 10, you should be able to:

  1. construct a two-by-two table comparing an index test with a reference classification;
  2. calculate and interpret sensitivity and specificity;
  3. calculate and interpret positive and negative predictive values;
  4. explain why predictive values change with disease prevalence even when sensitivity and specificity remain constant;
  5. distinguish sensitivity/specificity from predictive values by their conditioning direction;
  6. calculate and interpret likelihood ratios at an introductory level;
  7. explain how changing a test threshold trades sensitivity against specificity;
  8. interpret ROC curves and AUC conceptually without treating AUC as a complete measure of usefulness;
  9. compare serial and parallel testing strategies conceptually;
  10. distinguish diagnostic accuracy from agreement between tests;
  11. recognise verification, spectrum and imperfect-reference-standard problems;
  12. interpret a positive result in clinical and public-health context rather than as certainty of disease.

ORIENT · A positive test is not the same as disease

A screening programme uses a test with 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity in a population where disease prevalence is 1%.

Many students hear “95% specificity” and expect nearly every positive test to be a true case.

That intuition is wrong because the number of false positives also depends on how many people without disease are tested.

Diagnostic interpretation is a problem of conditional probability.

LEARN 1 · Build the diagnostic two-by-two table

Start with true disease status determined by the best available reference classification.

Disease + Disease −
Test + True positive (TP) False positive (FP)
Test − False negative (FN) True negative (TN)

The attached CDC material uses the same logic for surveillance-system sensitivity, specificity and predictive value positive.

The denominator determines the question being answered.

LEARN 2 · Sensitivity

Sensitivity asks:

Among people who truly have the condition, what proportion does the test identify as positive?

\[ Sensitivity = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]

High sensitivity means relatively few false negatives.

Sensitivity is useful when missing true cases carries important consequences, but maximising sensitivity alone can create many false positives if specificity falls.

LEARN 3 · Specificity

Specificity asks:

Among people who truly do not have the condition, what proportion does the test correctly classify as negative?

\[ Specificity = \frac{TN}{FP+TN} \]

High specificity means relatively few false positives.

Sensitivity and specificity condition on true disease status, not on the observed test result.

LEARN 4 · Predictive values reverse the conditioning direction

Positive predictive value (PPV) asks:

Among those who test positive, what proportion truly have the condition?

\[ PPV = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} \]

Negative predictive value (NPV) asks:

Among those who test negative, what proportion truly do not have the condition?

\[ NPV = \frac{TN}{FN+TN} \]

Source boundary The attached CDC source explicitly develops predictive value positive in the surveillance context. NPV is included here as a LIPPS diagnostic-testing extension.

LEARN 5 · Why prevalence changes PPV

Consider 10,000 people with 1% prevalence.

There are 100 true cases and 9,900 non-cases.

With 90% sensitivity:

  • TP = 90
  • FN = 10

With 95% specificity:

  • TN = 9,405
  • FP = 495

So:

\[ PPV = \frac{90}{90+495} \approx 15.4\% \]

Despite good sensitivity and specificity, only about 15% of positive results are true positives in this low-prevalence setting.

As prevalence rises, PPV usually rises and NPV usually falls, holding test properties constant.

This is why “the test is 95% accurate” is usually an unhelpful sentence.

LEARN 6 · Sensitivity and PPV answer different questions

This distinction should become automatic.

Sensitivity: start with people who truly have disease → ask how many test positive.

PPV: start with people whose test is positive → ask how many truly have disease.

The same true-positive cell appears in both formulas, but the denominators differ.

Confusing these quantities leads directly to poor patient counselling and poor screening-policy interpretation.

LEARN 7 · Likelihood ratios

LIPPS extension Likelihood ratios are not developed in the attached CDC source.

Likelihood ratios describe how much a test result changes the odds of disease.

\[ LR+ = \frac{Sensitivity}{1-Specificity} \]

\[ LR- = \frac{1-Sensitivity}{Specificity} \]

A large LR+ provides stronger evidence toward disease; a small LR− provides stronger evidence against disease.

Likelihood ratios combine sensitivity and specificity and can be used with pre-test odds to obtain post-test odds.

LEARN 8 · Thresholds create trade-offs

Many tests begin with a continuous value: blood pressure, biomarker concentration, risk score or optical density. A threshold converts that value into positive/negative classification.

Lowering a threshold usually:

  • identifies more true cases → sensitivity rises;
  • also labels more non-cases positive → specificity falls.

Raising the threshold usually does the reverse.

There is no universally “best” threshold. The appropriate choice depends on the consequences of false negatives and false positives, disease prevalence, downstream testing capacity and purpose of screening.

LEARN 9 · ROC curves and AUC

LIPPS extension ROC analysis is included as an advanced introductory extension.

A receiver operating characteristic curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity across thresholds.

The area under the curve (AUC) summarises discrimination: how well the test tends to rank diseased individuals above non-diseased individuals.

AUC does not tell you:

  • PPV in your population;
  • whether a chosen threshold is clinically useful;
  • whether the test is calibrated;
  • whether harms and costs are acceptable.

A good AUC is not the same as a good screening programme.

LEARN 10 · Serial and parallel testing

LIPPS extension Multi-test algorithms are not developed in the attached source.

Serial testing: a second test is performed or required after the first positive. This tends to increase overall specificity but can reduce sensitivity.

Parallel testing: a person may be classified positive if either of two tests is positive. This tends to increase sensitivity but can reduce specificity.

The exact combined performance depends on test dependence and algorithm rules. Do not mechanically multiply test characteristics without checking assumptions.

LEARN 11 · Agreement is not accuracy

Two tests can agree with each other and both be wrong if neither is compared with an adequate reference standard.

Accuracy questions ask how a test corresponds to true disease status.

Agreement questions ask how two raters or tests correspond to each other.

Measures such as percent agreement and kappa address agreement, not necessarily diagnostic truth.

LIPPS extension Agreement and kappa are not developed in the attached CDC lesson.

LEARN 12 · What could fool us in a diagnostic study?

Important threats include:

Spectrum effects — performance differs across disease severity or patient mix.

Verification bias — only some participants, often those with positive index tests, receive the reference standard.

Imperfect reference standard — the “gold standard” itself misclassifies disease.

Incorporation bias — the index test contributes to the reference diagnosis.

Reader or review bias — interpreters know other test results.

These problems change what apparent sensitivity and specificity mean.

A diagnostic-accuracy estimate belongs to the population, threshold, specimen process and reference standard under which it was obtained.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A fictional typhoid screening study tests 2,000 febrile patients. A reference classification identifies 200 true disease cases. The index test is positive in 170 of those 200 cases and positive in 270 of the 1,800 non-cases.

  1. Construct the 2×2 table.
  2. Calculate sensitivity.
  3. Calculate specificity.
  4. Calculate PPV.
  5. Calculate NPV.
  6. Calculate LR+ and LR−.
  7. Explain why PPV would differ if the same sensitivity and specificity were applied in a much lower-prevalence general population.
  8. If the screening threshold is lowered, predict the usual direction of change in sensitivity and specificity.
  9. Explain why a high percent agreement with another rapid test would not establish accuracy.
  10. Identify how the study could be biased if only index-test-positive patients received the reference test.
  11. Write a counselling statement that avoids telling every positive patient that they “have typhoid” with certainty.

GRADUATE DEEPEN · From test accuracy to clinical and public-health consequences

Sensitivity and specificity are only the beginning. A test is useful when its results change decisions in a population where those decisions improve outcomes relative to their costs and harms.

Bayesian updating with likelihood ratios

Pre-test odds are converted to post-test odds by:

\[ Posttest\ odds = Pretest\ odds \times LR \]

where:

\[ LR^+=\frac{Sensitivity}{1-Specificity},\qquad LR^-=\frac{1-Sensitivity}{Specificity} \]

This makes prevalence or prior probability explicit. A positive result from the same assay can imply very different post-test probabilities in a high-risk referral clinic versus a low-prevalence screening programme.

ROC curves are not decision curves

The ROC curve summarises sensitivity and specificity across thresholds. The area under the curve describes ranking/discrimination, not calibration, clinical benefit or the best threshold for a programme.

A model can discriminate well while producing poorly calibrated probabilities. Conversely, a modest change in AUC can still be clinically useful if it moves patients across a meaningful treatment threshold.

Spectrum effects

Accuracy depends on who is tested. A case-control accuracy study comparing obvious severe cases with exceptionally healthy controls can overestimate performance relative to routine practice, where disease severity and alternative diagnoses form a broader spectrum.

Verification and incorporation bias

If only participants with positive index tests receive the reference standard, estimates can be biased. If the index test forms part of the reference standard, agreement is partly built into the definition of truth. Blinding and prespecified handling of indeterminate results also matter.

Comparing two tests

When two tests are applied to the same participants, their results are paired. Analyses should preserve that pairing rather than compare sensitivity estimates as though they came from independent samples.

Imperfect reference standards

Some conditions have no error-free gold standard. Options include composite reference standards, expert adjudication, follow-up information or latent-class approaches, but each adds assumptions. Report the intended use and clinical role of the index test rather than describing accuracy in the abstract.

STARD and transparent reporting

STARD 2015 asks diagnostic-accuracy studies to report participant selection, index and reference test methods, prespecified thresholds, blinding, handling of indeterminate and missing results, variability analyses, sample-size rationale and precision of accuracy estimates.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. A test detects 90 of 100 true cases. What is its sensitivity?
  2. Why does specificity use non-diseased people in its denominator?
  3. How is PPV conceptually different from sensitivity?
  4. Why can PPV be low in a low-prevalence setting despite high specificity?
  5. What usually happens to sensitivity when the positive threshold is lowered?
  6. Does high AUC guarantee a high PPV in a low-prevalence population?
