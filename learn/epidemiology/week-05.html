LIPPS LEARN · EPIDEMIOLOGY · WEEK 05

Cross-sectional Studies

A snapshot can describe a population very well — but it can blur the direction of time.

Foundation Cross-sectional Prevalence Survey reasoning

This week in one sentence

Use cross-sectional designs to estimate prevalence and describe populations while recognising the limits created by simultaneous measurement of exposure and outcome.

The LIPPS lens
Evidence becomes useful when you can explain not only what the analysis shows, but also what the design, measurement and comparison allow you to claim.

Learning outcomes

By the end of Week 5, you should be able to:

  1. define a cross-sectional study from the timing of exposure and outcome measurement;
  2. explain why cross-sectional studies naturally estimate prevalence rather than incidence;
  3. distinguish target population, study population, sampling frame, sampling unit and observation unit;
  4. identify when a census or sample is appropriate;
  5. recognise how selection and non-response can alter prevalence estimates;
  6. identify when survey weights, clustering and stratification affect analysis;
  7. distinguish a prevalence ratio, prevalence difference and odds ratio as alternative ways to compare cross-sectional outcomes;
  8. explain the temporality problem when exposure and outcome are measured at the same time;
  9. recognise how disease duration can influence observed prevalence;
  10. write conclusions that match what a cross-sectional design can and cannot establish.

ORIENT · The survey found an association. What happened first?

A university surveys 2,000 students during one semester. Current depressive symptoms and current heavy social-media use are measured in the same questionnaire. Heavy users have more depressive symptoms.

What can the study establish?

It can estimate the prevalence of each measured state and quantify their association at the time of measurement. But unless the timing of exposure and outcome is reconstructed with credible additional information, the design does not tell us whether heavy social-media use preceded depressive symptoms, whether symptoms changed social-media use, or whether another factor influenced both.

That is the central discipline of cross-sectional interpretation: a strong snapshot is still a snapshot.

LEARN 1 · What makes a study cross-sectional?

The defining feature is not simply “a questionnaire.” It is the measurement structure: a sample is observed at a point or short period, and exposure and outcome status are measured at essentially the same time.

Cross-sectional studies can use interviews, records, examinations, laboratory tests or several sources together. A health-facility readiness assessment can also be cross-sectional even though the unit is a facility rather than a person.

The attached CDC source characterises cross-sectional studies as simultaneous measurement of exposures and health outcomes and notes their value for documenting prevalence.

LEARN 2 · The natural frequency measure is prevalence

If a survey asks who currently has hypertension, the numerator includes people whose hypertension began yesterday and people who have lived with it for years.

That is prevalence:

\[ P = \frac{\text{existing cases at the specified time}}{\text{population observed at that time}} \]

A cross-sectional study generally cannot estimate the probability of developing hypertension during a prior period unless incidence information was separately and reliably reconstructed.

This distinction matters because prevalence reflects both incidence and duration. A condition can have high prevalence because many new cases occur, because people live with it for a long time, or both.

LEARN 3 · Population language must be precise

A survey can be technically competent and still answer the wrong population question.

Target population — the population about which you ultimately want to make an inference.
Study population — the population from which study participants can actually be drawn under the protocol.
Sampling frame — the operational list or mechanism used to select units.
Sampling unit — the unit selected at a given sampling stage.
Observation unit — the unit on which measurements are ultimately recorded.

Example: a household survey may target adults in a district, select villages first, households second, and one adult within each household. The village and household are sampling units at different stages; the adult is the observation unit.

If the sampling frame excludes remote settlements, the “district estimate” may not represent the whole district even when the sample size is large.

LEARN 4 · A sample becomes an estimate only through its design

Simple random sampling is conceptually straightforward because each sampled unit has a known selection probability. Many public-health surveys, however, use stratified, cluster or multistage designs.

Those design features can affect:

  • weights, because some units have different selection probabilities;
  • standard errors, because people sampled within the same cluster may be correlated;
  • precision, because stratification and clustering change the information structure; and
  • population estimates, when weighted and unweighted distributions differ.

LIPPS extension
The attached CDC chapter defines cross-sectional surveys but does not provide a full complex-survey analysis framework. Survey weights, clustering and stratification are included here because they are essential for analysing modern population surveys correctly.

LEARN 5 · Non-response is not just a smaller sample

Suppose 80% of sampled adults participate. The main question is not merely whether 20% were lost; it is whether non-participants differ systematically from participants on variables related to the outcome.

If people with severe illness are less able to participate, prevalence may be underestimated. If people particularly interested in the topic are more likely to respond, prevalence may be overestimated.

A large final sample does not automatically remove non-response bias. Bias is about systematic difference, not simply sample size.

LEARN 6 · Comparing prevalence

Two groups can be compared using absolute or relative measures.

If hypertension prevalence is 30% in Group A and 20% in Group B:

Prevalence difference

\[ PD = 0.30 - 0.20 = 0.10 \]

There are 10 additional prevalent cases per 100 people in Group A.

Prevalence ratio

\[ PR = 0.30 / 0.20 = 1.50 \]

Prevalence in Group A is 1.5 times that in Group B.

LIPPS extension
Prevalence ratio and prevalence difference are included as modern cross-sectional effect measures. They are not explicitly developed in the attached CDC cross-sectional section.

LEARN 7 · Why the odds ratio can be awkward for common outcomes

An odds ratio can be calculated from cross-sectional binary data, and logistic regression often produces one naturally. But odds are not risks or prevalences.

When an outcome is common, an odds ratio can be numerically much farther from 1 than the corresponding prevalence ratio. That does not make logistic regression invalid; it means the estimand and wording must be correct.

If the scientific question is about relative prevalence, a prevalence ratio may be easier to communicate. If the model estimates odds, report odds honestly.

LEARN 8 · Temporality: the fundamental causal limitation

For many causal questions, the exposure must precede the outcome.

Cross-sectional simultaneous measurement often leaves that order uncertain. This produces several possibilities:

  • exposure → outcome;
  • outcome → exposure (reverse causation);
  • a third factor → both; or
  • measurement artifacts producing the association.

A survey showing that people with chronic back pain exercise less cannot by itself establish whether low exercise increased pain risk or pain reduced exercise.

Additional temporal questions can help, but retrospective recall introduces its own error. The design should still be described honestly.

LEARN 9 · Prevalence can select survivors

Because prevalence includes existing cases, it tends to over-represent conditions or cases of longer duration.

Imagine two diseases with the same incidence. Disease A resolves in days; Disease B persists for years. A cross-sectional survey is much more likely to capture Disease B.

This is sometimes called length or prevalence-incidence bias in broader epidemiological literature.

LIPPS extension
The attached text explains that prevalence depends on incidence and duration. The explicit survival-selection interpretation is developed here as an instructional extension.

LEARN 10 · Cross-sectional does not mean weak

“Cannot establish temporal sequence well” is not the same as “useless.”

Cross-sectional designs are excellent for questions such as:

  • What proportion of health facilities currently have oxygen?
  • What is the prevalence of hypertension among adults?
  • What proportion of adolescents report a behaviour?
  • How does current coverage vary by district?
  • Which groups currently have the greatest burden?

The strength of a design is judged against the question it was designed to answer. A cross-sectional survey can be the ideal design for prevalence and service-readiness questions.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A fictional district household survey samples 1,800 adults using 30 villages as first-stage clusters. Current hypertension is measured using a standard protocol. Current physical activity, medication use, age, sex and residence are also recorded.

Hypertension prevalence is 28%. Prevalence is 36% among adults reporting low physical activity and 22% among those reporting higher activity.

Before making a causal claim:

  1. Identify the target population, likely sampling units and observation unit.
  2. State the primary frequency measure.
  3. Calculate the crude prevalence ratio and prevalence difference.
  4. Explain why the crude association does not establish that low physical activity caused hypertension.
  5. List at least three plausible confounding or reverse-causation explanations.
  6. Explain why cluster sampling may affect the confidence interval.
  7. State what weighting information you would need to estimate district-level prevalence appropriately.
  8. Rewrite this statement: “Low physical activity increased hypertension by 64% in the district.”

A defensible version might say that low activity was associated with higher hypertension prevalence, while causal direction requires stronger temporal and confounding evidence.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. Why is prevalence the natural frequency measure in a typical cross-sectional survey?
  2. Can a cross-sectional study ever collect historical exposure information? If it does, does that automatically make the design longitudinal?
  3. What is the difference between the target population and the study population?
  4. Why can 5,000 poorly selected respondents produce a worse population estimate than 1,000 probability-sampled respondents?
  5. If prevalence is 40% versus 20%, what are the prevalence ratio and prevalence difference?
  6. Why might a chronic disease be easier to capture in a cross-sectional survey than a rapidly fatal disease?

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.
