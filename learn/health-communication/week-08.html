LIPPS LEARN · HEALTH COMMUNICATION · WEEK 08

Digital Health and Social Media Communication

Digital communication is fast, measurable and scalable—but its metrics can be seductive and its exclusions easy to ignore.

Social media Digital metrics Privacy Low bandwidth

This week in one sentence

Design and evaluate digital health communication while accounting for platform dynamics, privacy, accessibility, digital exclusion and the difference between engagement metrics and health outcomes.

The LIPPS lens
Good health communication asks not only whether a message was delivered, but who interpreted it, under what conditions, what action became possible, and what evidence supports the claimed effect.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. describe strengths and limitations of major digital communication formats;
  2. select digital platforms according to audience behaviour and objective;
  3. distinguish impressions, reach, engagement, click-through and conversion;
  4. explain algorithmic amplification and attention dynamics;
  5. design social-listening and moderation approaches;
  6. identify privacy, consent and confidentiality risks;
  7. apply mobile-first and low-bandwidth design principles;
  8. recognise digital exclusion and inequity;
  9. use digital analytics without overstating impact;
  10. identify appropriate roles and risks of automated and AI-assisted communication.

ORIENT · The campaign had two million impressions. Did it work?

A social-media campaign reports two million impressions, 60,000 likes and 12,000 shares. The objective was to increase uptake of adolescent HIV self-testing. The campaign does not track whether the intended age group saw the content, whether viewers understood where to obtain tests, or whether testing increased.

Digital platforms make some things easy to count. Easy-to-count metrics are not automatically the outcomes that matter.

LEARN 1 · Digital channels change speed, scale and interaction

Websites, SMS, messaging apps, social platforms, chatbots and mobile apps can support rapid dissemination, reminders, dialogue and tailored content. Their value depends on the communication task and user context.

LEARN 2 · Platform selection begins with audience behaviour

Do not choose a platform because it is fashionable. Ask who uses it, how often, for what purpose, at what cost, with what privacy, and whether the desired action can follow naturally from the platform experience.

LEARN 3 · Understand the metric ladder

Impressions count displays. Reach estimates unique users exposed. Engagement includes reactions, comments or shares. Click-through indicates movement to another resource. Conversion captures a defined action. Each metric is further from health impact than teams sometimes imply.

LEARN 4 · Algorithms shape visibility

Social platforms rank and recommend content using proprietary systems. Emotional, novel or controversial material can receive disproportionate attention. Organic reach can change without any change in message quality. Platform metrics should therefore be interpreted as products of both audience response and platform design.

LEARN 5 · Social listening is systematic observation, not surveillance of individuals

Social listening identifies recurring questions, concerns, narratives and information gaps in public or appropriately consented spaces. It should be conducted with a defined purpose, privacy safeguards and caution about representativeness. Online conversation does not equal population opinion.

LEARN 6 · Moderation is part of communication design

Comment spaces can contain genuine questions, abuse, misinformation, disclosure of personal health information or requests for clinical advice. Establish rules for response, escalation, deletion and crisis handling before the campaign launches.

LEARN 7 · Privacy can be harmed by seemingly harmless features

A reproductive-health SMS appearing on a shared phone, a geotagged HIV-service check-in, or a public reply from a clinic account can disclose sensitive information. Collect the minimum data required and consider who else may see notifications or account activity.

LEARN 8 · Mobile-first means more than shrinking a desktop page

Use readable text, large touch targets, compressed assets, captions, alt text, transcripts and forms that work on low-cost devices. A low-bandwidth path should preserve the essential message and action even when video or interactive content fails.

LEARN 9 · Digital exclusion is an epidemiological issue

If the people most likely to need an intervention are least likely to have data, devices, literacy or private access, digital-only communication can widen inequity. Monitor reach by meaningful subgroups and provide non-digital alternatives.

LEARN 10 · AI can assist, but human accountability remains

Automated drafting, translation, summarisation and chatbots can increase speed. They can also hallucinate, reproduce bias, mishandle sensitive data or provide unsafe clinical advice. Use human review, defined scope, source verification, privacy controls and escalation to qualified professionals.

DEEPEN · Digital communication changes the information environment

Digital platforms do more than transmit messages. They rank, recommend, personalise and amplify content. The same health message can therefore reach different people in different contexts, surrounded by comments, competing claims and platform-generated cues about popularity.

Platform affordances

Short-form video encourages different communication from a clinic portal or WhatsApp group. Design should consider discoverability, sharing, comment culture, privacy, persistence, moderation and whether users can ask questions.

Metrics hierarchy

Separate exposure metrics such as impressions and views from engagement metrics such as clicks and comments, communication outcomes such as comprehension or trust, and health or service outcomes such as vaccination or appointment completion. A viral post can be a communication failure if people share it because it is confusing or controversial.

Privacy and sensitive health information

A platform may expose a person’s interests, group membership or messages to employers, family members or advertisers. Do not assume that a direct message is private merely because it is not public. Collect the minimum data needed, explain its use and avoid unnecessary linkage of identity to sensitive health topics.

Digital exclusion

Device ownership, disability, data cost, network coverage, language and gendered control of phones influence who can benefit. Digital-first programmes should maintain meaningful alternatives rather than relegating offline audiences to an inferior service.

Moderation and community management

Comments can reveal confusion, misinformation and adverse experiences. Establish escalation rules for threats, clinical emergencies, abuse, personal data and misinformation. Moderation should distinguish disagreement from harmful conduct.

AI and automated communication

Chatbots can provide scalable answers but may generate incorrect or overconfident health information. High-risk outputs need constrained knowledge sources, transparent limitations, escalation to humans and monitoring. Automation should not masquerade as a clinician.

What could fool us?

Algorithms make platform data endogenous: the people who see a post are partly selected by prior behaviour and platform ranking. Comparing engagement across posts does not necessarily identify which message would work better in the whole population. Digital analytics are valuable operational data, not automatically causal evidence.

FIELD LAB · Build a digital metrics ladder

A useful dashboard separates what the platform did from what people understood and what happened next.

Level Example metric What it can tell you
exposure unique reach, impressions whether content was delivered
engagement clicks, shares, comments whether users interacted
communication comprehension, trust, confidence whether the message changed a proximal communication outcome
behaviour appointment completion, hotline use whether the intended action occurred
health/service diagnosis, treatment initiation downstream programme outcome

Do not skip levels when interpreting results. A high click rate with low comprehension indicates a different problem from low reach.

Digital safety review

Before launch, answer: What personal data are collected? Is the channel private enough for the topic? Can users opt out? What happens if someone reports self-harm, abuse or an emergency? Who moderates harmful comments? How are AI-generated answers constrained and audited? What non-digital alternative exists?

A/B testing caution

Randomised message testing can improve subject lines or calls to action, but optimise for the right outcome. A headline that maximises clicks by provoking fear may reduce trust. The optimisation metric is part of the ethical design.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A youth-friendly service plans a WhatsApp and Instagram campaign offering information about STI testing. Young people report concerns about privacy, some share phones with family, rural data costs are high, and staff cannot answer messages 24 hours a day.

  1. Define the communication objective and conversion event.
  2. Decide what should be public content versus private interaction.
  3. Specify privacy safeguards for notifications and direct messages.
  4. Design a low-bandwidth alternative.
  5. Create a moderation and escalation policy for clinical questions and abuse.
  6. Select five digital metrics and state what each can and cannot prove.
  7. Explain how social listening would be conducted ethically.
  8. Identify where AI assistance could be useful and where human review is mandatory.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. What is the difference between impressions and reach?
  2. Why does engagement not prove behaviour change?
  3. Give one privacy risk of health communication through messaging apps.
  4. Why is online social listening not automatically representative of the population?
  5. What makes a digital experience genuinely low-bandwidth?
  6. Name two safeguards for AI-assisted health communication.

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.

DEMONSTRATE · Week 8 mastery quiz

Pilot Mastery Quiz: 10 objective questions randomly sampled from a curated Week 8 bank of 30 items.

Initial time limit: 12 minutes. Mode: one question at a time; randomised item selection; answer options shuffled; limited attempts; feedback released according to course or institutional settings.

Assessment principle
Knowing the material should be the fastest strategy. Timing is calibrated to reward prepared reading and reasoning, not to replace sound question design or approved accessibility accommodations.
