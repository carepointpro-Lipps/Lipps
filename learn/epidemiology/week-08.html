LIPPS LEARN · EPIDEMIOLOGY · WEEK 08

Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs

Stronger causal inference comes from better counterfactual comparison, not from the word “intervention.”

Intermediate Trials Randomisation Quasi-experiments

This week in one sentence

Distinguish randomised experiments from observational and quasi-experimental evaluations and judge how allocation and comparison design affect causal interpretation.

The LIPPS lens
Evidence becomes useful when you can explain not only what the analysis shows, but also what the design, measurement and comparison allow you to claim.

Learning outcomes

By the end of Week 8, you should be able to:

  1. distinguish experimental from observational epidemiological studies;
  2. explain what random allocation is intended to achieve;
  3. distinguish individual and cluster allocation conceptually;
  4. explain the roles of control groups, allocation concealment and blinding at an introductory level;
  5. distinguish efficacy from effectiveness;
  6. recognise common threats created by non-adherence and loss to follow-up;
  7. explain intention-to-treat conceptually;
  8. distinguish simple before-after evaluation from stronger controlled or interrupted designs;
  9. identify when randomisation is unethical, infeasible or unnecessary;
  10. write causal conclusions in proportion to the strength of the design and execution.

ORIENT · An intervention happened and outcomes improved

A district introduces a new maternal referral programme. Facility deliveries rise from 58% before implementation to 72% afterwards.

Did the programme cause the increase?

Not necessarily. Other things may have changed at the same time: transport, staffing, reporting, population composition, another campaign or a secular trend.

An intervention is not automatically an experiment, and a before-after difference is not automatically an effect.

LEARN 1 · What makes a study experimental?

The attached CDC text distinguishes experimental from observational studies by whether the investigator determines the exposure or intervention through a controlled process.

In a clinical trial, participants may be assigned to a new intervention or a comparison condition and followed for outcomes. In community trials, the unit of intervention can be a group or community.

The analytical logic remains comparison, but assignment creates an opportunity for stronger causal inference.

LEARN 2 · Why randomisation matters

Random allocation gives eligible units a chance of receiving each study condition according to a random mechanism.

Its main causal advantage is not that the groups become identical. It is that, with adequate implementation and sample size, both measured and unmeasured baseline causes of the outcome are expected to be balanced on average, apart from chance.

Randomisation therefore protects against systematic treatment selection.

A single randomised study can still have baseline imbalance by chance; randomisation is a process, not a guarantee of perfect symmetry.

LEARN 3 · The comparison group remains essential

Without a comparison group, a change after intervention may reflect:

  • natural recovery;
  • regression to the mean;
  • secular trends;
  • seasonal change;
  • another intervention;
  • changing measurement or reporting; or
  • changes in who is observed.

Randomised trials create a contemporaneous comparison intended to represent what would have happened without the intervention.

That counterfactual logic is the real source of causal strength.

LEARN 4 · Allocation concealment and blinding

LIPPS extension These trial-quality concepts are not developed in detail in the attached introductory CDC chapter.

Allocation concealment prevents those enrolling participants from predicting the next assignment, reducing selection manipulation.

Blinding prevents selected participants, clinicians, outcome assessors or analysts from knowing assignment when that knowledge could alter behaviour or measurement.

Blinding is not always possible. A health worker usually knows whether a new workflow has been introduced. The design should then focus on objective outcomes, standardised measurement and blinded outcome assessment where feasible.

LEARN 5 · Individual versus cluster randomisation

Sometimes an intervention is delivered to groups rather than individuals—for example, a facility-level triage protocol or village-level health campaign.

Randomising whole facilities or communities can reduce contamination but creates correlation among people within the same cluster.

LIPPS extension Cluster-randomised trials require sample-size and analysis methods that account for clustering. Treating all individuals as independent can overstate precision.

LEARN 6 · Efficacy versus effectiveness

The attached CDC source distinguishes:

Efficacy — performance under ideal or controlled conditions.

Effectiveness — performance under typical field conditions.

The difference is not merely vocabulary. It changes what population, adherence, delivery conditions and implementation problems the estimate represents.

A highly efficacious intervention can have modest real-world effectiveness when uptake, adherence, supply or implementation is poor.

LEARN 7 · Non-adherence and intention-to-treat

LIPPS extension Intention-to-treat is introduced here because it is fundamental to interpreting randomised trials.

Participants do not always follow their assigned intervention. Analysing only those who complied can recreate confounding because compliers may differ systematically from non-compliers.

Intention-to-treat analysis compares groups according to original random assignment, preserving the causal protection of randomisation for the effect of assignment.

Per-protocol questions can also be scientifically useful, but they require additional assumptions and should not casually replace the randomised comparison.

LEARN 8 · When randomisation is not feasible

Randomisation may be unethical when an intervention is already known to be beneficial, politically unacceptable, operationally impossible, or inappropriate for a system-wide policy already being implemented.

Researchers then use observational or quasi-experimental designs.

The key question becomes:

How can we construct the most credible comparison possible without random assignment?

LEARN 9 · Quasi-experimental designs

LIPPS extension The attached CDC introduction does not provide a detailed quasi-experimental taxonomy. This section is an applied extension.

Simple before-after: compare outcome before and after intervention. Easy but highly vulnerable to time-related confounding.

Controlled before-after: add a comparable non-intervention group and examine whether change differs between groups.

Interrupted time series: use many repeated observations before and after an intervention to estimate changes in level or trend.

Controlled interrupted time series: add a comparison series to strengthen attribution.

Other advanced quasi-experimental strategies exist, but the principle remains: exploit design structure to approximate the counterfactual more credibly.

LEARN 10 · Design does not compensate for poor execution

A randomised label cannot save:

  • major loss to follow-up;
  • differential outcome measurement;
  • contamination;
  • protocol changes;
  • selective reporting;
  • poor adherence information; or
  • analysis that ignores the unit of randomisation.

Conversely, a well-designed quasi-experiment can sometimes provide more credible evidence than a badly executed trial.

Judge the actual comparison created, not only the design name.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A health ministry plans to introduce a new infection-prevention coaching package in 24 hospitals. Political leaders want all hospitals eventually to receive it, but implementation capacity allows only 12 to start immediately.

Consider three possible evaluations:

A. Compare the 12 early hospitals before versus after implementation.
B. Randomly select which 12 receive the package first and compare outcomes with the waiting hospitals during the same period.
C. Use 24 monthly observations before and 12 after national rollout, with no concurrent control.

For each option:

  1. Name the design.
  2. Describe its comparison or counterfactual logic.
  3. Identify its strongest threat to causal interpretation.
  4. State what outcome-measurement features would reduce bias.
  5. Explain what clustering means if patient-level outcomes are analysed.
  6. Distinguish efficacy from effectiveness in this programme.
  7. Explain why analysing only hospitals that implemented the package perfectly might bias the effect estimate.
  8. Rank the three designs for causal strength under otherwise good execution and justify your ranking.

GRADUATE DEEPEN · Trials, estimands and quasi-experimental alternatives

Randomisation protects against confounding at assignment, but trials still require careful decisions about adherence, missing outcomes, clustering and the effect actually being estimated.

Intention-to-treat and per-protocol questions

An intention-to-treat (ITT) effect compares groups according to randomised assignment regardless of subsequent adherence. It preserves the randomisation and often answers a policy question about offering or assigning an intervention.

A per-protocol effect asks what would happen if participants adhered to a specified protocol. A naïve analysis that simply compares adherers with non-adherers usually destroys randomisation because adherence is itself associated with prognosis. Causal per-protocol analyses require adjustment for predictors of adherence and censoring.

Cluster-randomised trials

When schools, facilities or villages are randomised, outcomes within clusters are correlated. Sample-size calculations and analysis must account for the intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC). A large number of participants in very few clusters does not create the same information as many independent clusters.

Stepped-wedge designs

In a stepped-wedge cluster trial, clusters cross from control to intervention at different scheduled periods. The design can be attractive when phased rollout is operationally necessary, but secular time trends become inseparable from intervention rollout unless the design and analysis account for calendar time.

Difference-in-differences

A difference-in-differences analysis compares changes over time between intervention and comparison groups. Its central identification assumption is not that groups have equal baseline levels, but that they would have followed sufficiently similar trends in the absence of intervention.

Examine pre-intervention trends and avoid treating one pre-period and one post-period as automatic proof of parallel trends.

Interrupted time series

Interrupted time series uses repeated observations before and after an intervention to estimate changes in level and/or slope. It is stronger than a simple before-after comparison because it models the pre-intervention trajectory. Threats include concurrent interventions, seasonality, autocorrelation and changes in measurement.

Regression discontinuity and instrumental variables

These designs exploit special assignment mechanisms:

  • Regression discontinuity uses a treatment rule based on a threshold, such as eligibility below a CD4 count cutoff.
  • Instrumental-variable methods use a variable that changes exposure but affects the outcome only through that exposure under strong assumptions.

Both can support causal inference when assumptions are credible; neither is a shortcut around subject-matter reasoning.

Reporting standard

For randomised trials, CONSORT 2025 supersedes CONSORT 2010 and emphasises transparent reporting of design, analysis, participant flow, missing data, open-science elements and how interventions were actually delivered.
