LIPPS LEARN · HEALTH COMMUNICATION · WEEK 12

Ethics, Equity and the Future of Health Communication

Effective communication that manipulates, exposes or excludes people is not good health communication.

Ethics Equity AI Capstone

This week in one sentence

Evaluate health communication for ethical integrity, equity, accessibility and responsible use of emerging technologies, then integrate the full course using the LIPPS SIGNAL framework.

The LIPPS lens
Good health communication asks not only whether a message was delivered, but who interpreted it, under what conditions, what action became possible, and what evidence supports the claimed effect.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. apply autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice and truthfulness to communication decisions;
  2. distinguish persuasion from manipulation and coercion;
  3. identify stigma, fear and dignity risks in campaign design;
  4. assess privacy and data-protection risks in digital communication;
  5. design communication for equity and inclusion;
  6. recognise representation and power issues in imagery and storytelling;
  7. identify risks of AI-generated and personalised health communication;
  8. specify human oversight and verification requirements for AI use;
  9. evaluate unintended effects and conflicts of interest;
  10. apply the LIPPS SIGNAL framework to a complete communication problem.

ORIENT · High reach, low dignity

A campaign to encourage HIV testing uses images of visibly ill people and the slogan “Do not be the one who infects your family.” Reach is high and testing increases briefly, but people living with HIV report increased stigma and some avoid clinics because they fear being recognised.

A communication programme should not be judged only by whether it changes behaviour. Ethical harms and unequal burdens are outcomes too.

LEARN 1 · Ethics applies to both content and process

Health communication should respect autonomy, promote benefit, minimise avoidable harm and distribute benefits and burdens fairly. Truthfulness, transparency and respect for persons are especially important because health messages can influence intimate decisions.

LEARN 2 · Persuasion has limits

Public health legitimately tries to encourage protective behaviour. Persuasion becomes ethically problematic when it relies on deception, hidden sponsorship, humiliation, coercive threats or manipulation that undermines informed choice. Urgency does not remove the need for honesty.

LEARN 3 · Fear can produce harm

Fear appeals may attract attention, but exaggerated threats, shame or blame can increase stigma, fatalism or avoidance. When serious risk is communicated, pair it with clear, feasible protective action and avoid assigning moral worth to disease status.

LEARN 4 · Images and stories carry power

Consent for a photograph does not automatically make every future use appropriate. Consider dignity, context, re-identification, unequal power and whether stories portray communities only as victims. Representation should avoid tokenism and stereotypes.

LEARN 5 · Privacy is part of communication ethics

Digital targeting, message history, location data, chat transcripts and audience segmentation can reveal sensitive health information. Use data minimisation, purpose limitation, secure handling and clear consent where applicable. Do not collect personal data merely because analytics make it possible.

LEARN 6 · Equity means more than translating a poster

Ask which groups have less access to information, trusted messengers, digital devices, literacy support or health services. Universal design, accessible formats, local languages, low-bandwidth options and alternative channels can reduce exclusion.

LEARN 7 · AI creates speed and new failure modes

Generative AI can draft, translate, summarise and personalise communication. It can also invent facts, reproduce stereotypes, expose confidential information, produce unsafe medical advice and create convincing synthetic media. High-stakes health communication requires verified sources and accountable human review.

LEARN 8 · Personalisation can become manipulation or surveillance

Targeting messages based on behaviour or inferred vulnerability can improve relevance but may become intrusive, discriminatory or exploitative. Ask whether people reasonably expect the data use, whether sensitive attributes are inferred, and whether the targeting could harm them.

LEARN 9 · Conflicts of interest should be visible

Audiences should know when sponsors, commercial interests or political incentives may affect communication. Hidden sponsorship can undermine trust even when the factual content is correct.

LEARN 10 · LIPPS SIGNAL: a capstone planning framework

Use S — Situation: define the health and communication problem. I — Intended audience and insight: identify who must decide and what evidence says about context. G — Goal: specify the communication objective and intended mechanism. N — Narrative and needed action: decide what people need to understand, feel or do. A — Access: select messenger, channel, timing, accessibility and service pathway. L — Listen and learn: build feedback, monitoring, evaluation and adaptation.

SIGNAL is a LIPPS synthesis for structuring—not replacing—more detailed programme planning.

LEARN 11 · Final test: does the communication earn its effect ethically?

A strong plan should be understandable, evidence-based, feasible, inclusive, respectful and measurable. Ask not only “Will this work?” but also “For whom?”, “At what cost?”, “Who could be harmed?”, and “What would make us change course?”

DEEPEN · Ethical communication is part of effectiveness

Communication that achieves a short-term behaviour change by humiliating, deceiving or excluding people is not high-quality public-health communication. Ethics should be built into problem definition, audience research, message design, data collection, targeting, evaluation and technology choices.

Autonomy and truthful persuasion

Health communication often aims to influence behaviour. Ethical persuasion provides relevant evidence, explains uncertainty and respects the person’s ability to decide. Manipulation hides material information, exploits vulnerability or creates a false impression of certainty.

Stigma and fear

Fear can attract attention, but fear without efficacy can produce avoidance, denial or stigma. Messages about HIV, TB, sexually transmitted infections, obesity, mental health or outbreak risk should avoid implying that affected people are irresponsible or dangerous simply because of their condition.

Equity

Ask not only whether average outcomes improved but who was excluded, who carried new burdens and who benefited least. A digital campaign that improves access for urban smartphone users while reducing investment in other channels may widen inequity.

Privacy, targeting and data governance

Audience segmentation can become surveillance when sensitive data are collected without necessity or clear consent. Minimise data, restrict access, establish retention rules and avoid targeting that could reveal a person’s health status to others.

Generative AI and synthetic media

AI can support drafting, translation, summarisation and chat interfaces, but it can also fabricate evidence, introduce bias or create highly persuasive synthetic content. Human review is essential for health claims. Disclose material use of synthetic media where deception is plausible, and never invent patient testimony or community endorsement.

The LIPPS SIGNAL capstone

Use S — Situation, I — Intended audience and insight, G — Goal, N — Narrative and needed action, A — Access, L — Listen and learn as a final integration tool. SIGNAL is not a substitute for formal theory or evaluation; it is a disciplined checklist that keeps communication connected to a real decision and a feedback loop.

Final critical question

Before publishing, ask: If this communication works exactly as intended, could it still harm someone? Then consider stigma, privacy, exclusion, service capacity, misunderstanding and unintended incentives.

What could fool us?

Ethical review cannot rely only on the communicator’s intention. Harms may appear in subgroups that were absent from pre-testing. AI systems can behave differently after deployment than in demonstrations. Ethical communication therefore requires monitoring, incident reporting and willingness to revise or withdraw content.

FIELD LAB · Run an ethics and equity pre-mortem

Before launch, imagine the communication caused harm. Ask what the most plausible failure would have been.

Domain Pre-mortem question
autonomy could people be manipulated or materially misled?
stigma could a group be blamed, shamed or exposed?
equity who cannot access or act on the message?
privacy could targeting or notifications reveal health status?
service capacity could successful communication create demand the system cannot meet?
AI could automated content fabricate, discriminate or overstate certainty?
evidence are we claiming more than the evaluation design supports?

Document the mitigation before publication. For higher-risk interventions, assign an owner and review date to each mitigation.

SIGNAL worked example

For an adolescent mental-health helpline:

  • Situation: low use despite high reported distress.
  • Intended audience and insight: adolescents know the helpline exists but fear confidentiality breaches.
  • Goal: increase confidence that help-seeking is private and safe.
  • Narrative and needed action: explain confidentiality clearly and give a discreet way to initiate contact.
  • Access: provide channels that work on shared phones and for low-data users.
  • Listen and learn: monitor confidentiality questions, complaints, comprehension and actual use.

The framework reveals that the central issue is not awareness. It also forces the team to examine privacy and access before celebrating higher call volume.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A health authority wants to use an AI chatbot and targeted social-media advertising to increase uptake of mental-health services among university students. The advertising platform can target users inferred to be interested in depression-related content. The chatbot can answer common questions but occasionally gives overconfident clinical advice. Students fear that counselling use could become known to peers or administrators.

Apply LIPPS SIGNAL: 1. Define the situation and communication problem. 2. Identify intended audiences and required formative insight. 3. Write a measurable goal. 4. Define the narrative and action without stigmatising users. 5. Design access: channels, privacy, low-bandwidth options and escalation to human care. 6. Decide whether inferred mental-health targeting is ethically acceptable and justify your position. 7. Specify AI guardrails, source verification and human oversight. 8. Build listening and evaluation mechanisms. 9. Identify at least four possible unintended harms. 10. State the conditions under which the programme should be paused or redesigned.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. What is the difference between persuasion and coercion?
  2. Why can a campaign that changes behaviour still be ethically poor?
  3. Give one privacy risk of personalised digital health communication.
  4. What additional safeguard is needed when AI produces high-stakes health information?
  5. How does equity differ from giving everyone exactly the same communication?
  6. Apply the six letters of LIPPS SIGNAL to a simple health communication problem.

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.

DEMONSTRATE · Week 12 mastery quiz

Pilot Mastery Quiz: 10 objective questions randomly sampled from a curated Week 12 bank of 30 items.

Initial time limit: 15 minutes. Mode: one question at a time; randomised item selection; answer options shuffled; limited attempts; feedback released according to course or institutional settings.

Assessment principle
Knowing the material should be the fastest strategy. Timing is calibrated to reward prepared reading and reasoning, not to replace sound question design or approved accessibility accommodations.
