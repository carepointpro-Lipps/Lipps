LIPPS LEARN · EPIDEMIOLOGY · WEEK 07

Cohort Studies

Start with exposure, preserve the timeline, and watch what happens next.

Intermediate Cohort Incidence Follow-up

This week in one sentence

Use cohort designs to estimate incidence and compare outcome occurrence between exposure groups while accounting for follow-up, person-time and loss to observation.

The LIPPS lens
Evidence becomes useful when you can explain not only what the analysis shows, but also what the design, measurement and comparison allow you to claim.

Learning outcomes

By the end of Week 7, you should be able to:

  1. recognise a cohort study from exposure-based grouping and subsequent outcome ascertainment;
  2. distinguish prospective and retrospective cohort studies;
  3. explain why cohort studies can estimate incidence directly;
  4. calculate and interpret risk ratios and risk differences in cohort data;
  5. explain when person-time incidence rates are needed;
  6. describe censoring and unequal follow-up conceptually;
  7. recognise how loss to follow-up can reduce precision or introduce selection bias;
  8. choose an appropriate unexposed or comparison cohort;
  9. distinguish fixed from changing exposures conceptually;
  10. identify major strengths and limitations of cohort designs.

ORIENT · Exposure comes first

A cohort study starts with a group defined by exposure or another baseline characteristic and asks what happens afterwards.

Imagine 2,000 health workers without active tuberculosis at baseline. Half work in high-exposure clinical areas and half in lower-exposure settings. They are followed for two years and new tuberculosis diagnoses are recorded.

Because outcome-free people are observed over time, the design can estimate incidence, not merely prevalence.

LEARN 1 · The core structure

The attached CDC text describes cohort studies as observational designs in which participants are grouped by exposure and then followed to document disease occurrence.

Conceptually:

Exposed → outcome / no outcome
Unexposed → outcome / no outcome

The comparison group estimates what disease occurrence might have looked like without the exposure pattern of primary interest.

Unlike an experiment, the investigator does not assign exposure; the exposure occurs under natural or existing conditions.

LEARN 2 · Prospective and retrospective cohorts

In a prospective cohort, participants are enrolled before the outcome occurs and are followed forward.

In a retrospective cohort, both exposure and outcome may already have occurred when the study begins, but the logical sequence remains exposure → outcome. Existing records reconstruct the cohort and follow-up.

The attached CDC material emphasises this important point: retrospective does not mean case-control. A retrospective cohort still groups people by exposure and compares subsequent disease occurrence.

LEARN 3 · Cohorts estimate incidence directly

If all participants are outcome-free at baseline and follow-up is defined, you can calculate risk:

\[ Risk_E = \frac{\text{new cases among exposed}}{\text{exposed population at risk at baseline}} \]

and similarly for the unexposed group.

You can then calculate:

\[ RR = Risk_E / Risk_U \]

and:

\[ RD = Risk_E - Risk_U \]

These have direct interpretations because the denominators arise from actual populations at risk rather than investigator-selected numbers of cases and controls.

LEARN 4 · A worked risk comparison

Suppose 80 of 800 exposed workers develop a condition, compared with 20 of 1,000 unexposed workers.

\[ Risk_E = 80/800 = 10\% \]

\[ Risk_U = 20/1000 = 2\% \]

\[ RR = 10\%/2\% = 5.0 \]

\[ RD = 10\%-2\% = 8\text{ percentage points} \]

A careful interpretation is:

During the specified follow-up, exposed workers had five times the risk and eight additional cases per 100 workers compared with the unexposed group.

Whether that difference is causal depends on confounding, bias, measurement and the wider design.

LEARN 5 · Person-time when follow-up differs

Participants do not always contribute equal follow-up. Some enter late, migrate, die, withdraw or develop the outcome early.

Person-time accumulates the time each person remains under observation and at risk.

\[ Incidence\ rate = \frac{new\ cases}{total\ person-time\ at\ risk} \]

The attached CDC text explains that person-time is useful precisely because it can accommodate different observation periods.

A rate ratio then compares person-time incidence rates between groups.

LEARN 6 · Censoring is not the same as failure

A participant who leaves follow-up without developing the outcome does not necessarily become a case or a non-case for the whole intended period. Their observed disease-free time can still contribute information.

This is the idea behind censoring.

LIPPS extension
Formal survival-analysis methods are beyond this introductory week, but learners should understand that incomplete follow-up is handled through time-to-event methods rather than by pretending everyone was observed equally.

LEARN 7 · Loss to follow-up can create bias

Loss to follow-up first reduces information, but it becomes a threat to validity when loss is related to both exposure and outcome risk.

Example: if highly exposed workers who develop early symptoms are more likely to leave employment and disappear from records, the observed exposed cohort may look healthier than the true cohort.

Ask:

  • How much follow-up is missing?
  • Why did participants leave?
  • Does loss differ by exposure group?
  • Is outcome risk plausibly related to the reason for loss?

The percentage lost is important, but the mechanism of loss matters more.

LEARN 8 · Comparison cohort quality

An unexposed cohort should represent a credible baseline for the exposed group.

If exposed workers differ from unexposed workers in age, socioeconomic status, access to care or baseline disease risk, crude outcome differences may reflect those factors rather than exposure.

Restriction, matching, stratification and regression can sometimes address measured differences, but design-stage comparability remains fundamental.

LEARN 9 · Time-varying exposure

LIPPS extension Many real exposures change during follow-up. A participant may start medication, change occupation, stop smoking or move between exposure categories.

Treating a changing exposure as if it remained fixed can misclassify person-time and obscure temporal relationships.

At introductory level, the key question is simple:

Does the exposure value at baseline still represent the participant’s exposure throughout the period being analysed?

If not, a more advanced longitudinal analysis may be needed.

LEARN 10 · Strengths and limitations

Strengths

  • preserves temporal ordering more clearly than a typical cross-sectional design;
  • directly measures incidence;
  • can evaluate multiple outcomes from one exposure;
  • useful for rare exposures;
  • allows study of natural history and change over time.

Limitations

  • inefficient for very rare outcomes unless the cohort is huge;
  • prospective studies can be slow and expensive;
  • loss to follow-up can threaten validity;
  • exposure may change;
  • observational cohorts remain vulnerable to confounding.

A long follow-up does not automatically mean a strong study. The quality of measurement and comparison remains decisive.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A fictional cohort enrols 3,000 adults free of diabetes. At baseline, 1,000 have a high-risk occupational exposure and 2,000 do not. Over five years, 100 exposed and 80 unexposed participants develop diabetes. Follow-up time totals 4,200 person-years in the exposed group and 8,800 person-years in the unexposed group.

  1. Calculate cumulative risk in each group.
  2. Calculate the risk ratio and risk difference.
  3. Calculate incidence rates per 1,000 person-years.
  4. Calculate the rate ratio.
  5. Explain why risk ratio and rate ratio answer related but not identical questions.
  6. Suppose 20% of exposed participants with early symptoms leave employment and cannot be traced. Explain the potential direction of bias.
  7. Identify at least three baseline variables that could confound the association.
  8. Explain whether this observational cohort can establish causation merely because exposure was measured before disease.
  9. If exposure status changed for many participants, state what additional information should have been collected.
  10. Write a defensible conclusion.

GRADUATE DEEPEN · Time-to-event analysis, censoring and immortal time

Once follow-up varies across people, a single risk at the end of study can hide important information. Survival analysis treats when an event occurs as part of the outcome.

Survival and hazard are different quantities

The survival function is:

\[ S(t)=P(T>t) \]

It gives the probability of remaining event-free beyond time \(t\).

The hazard describes the instantaneous event rate among those still event-free. A hazard ratio therefore compares hazards, not cumulative risks. Even when hazards are proportional, a hazard ratio of 0.70 does not literally mean a 30% lower risk at every time point.

Kaplan-Meier estimation

The Kaplan-Meier estimator updates survival whenever an event occurs and can accommodate right censoring. It assumes that, conditional on the information used, censored participants are sufficiently comparable to those remaining under observation. If people leave because they are deteriorating and that process is not addressed, the estimate can be biased.

Cox proportional-hazards model

The Cox model relates covariates to the hazard without specifying the baseline hazard:

\[ h(t|X)=h_0(t)\exp(\beta X) \]

The proportional-hazards assumption implies a stable hazard ratio over time. Check it rather than merely report a Cox model because software made it convenient. If the effect changes over time, consider time interactions, flexible models or reporting time-specific absolute risks.

Competing risks

If death prevents the outcome of interest, standard Kaplan-Meier methods that censor deaths can overstate the real-world probability of that outcome. Decide whether your scientific question concerns a cause-specific hazard or the cumulative incidence of the outcome in the presence of competing events.

Immortal-time bias

Immortal time is a period during which, by design, a participant must remain outcome-free to be classified as exposed. Suppose “treated” means receiving a drug within 90 days after diagnosis, but follow-up begins at diagnosis. Anyone who dies on day 20 can never become treated, while those eventually treated must survive long enough to receive treatment. Misclassifying the pre-treatment period as exposed creates an artificial survival advantage.

Time-varying exposure coding or explicit target-trial alignment can prevent this bias.

Loss to follow-up as informative censoring

Instead of reporting only the percentage lost, model the causal structure of loss. Ask whether determinants of dropout also predict the outcome and whether they differ across exposure groups. In advanced work, inverse-probability-of-censoring weights can be used under explicit assumptions.
