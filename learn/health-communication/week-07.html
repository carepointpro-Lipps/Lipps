LIPPS LEARN · HEALTH COMMUNICATION · WEEK 07

Communication Channels and Media Strategy

The best channel is not the newest channel. It is the channel that reaches the right people, at the right moment, with enough credibility and opportunity for action.

Channels Media mix Reach Messengers

This week in one sentence

Select and combine communication channels and messengers according to audience access, trust, objective, reach, frequency, interactivity, cost and decision timing.

The LIPPS lens
Good health communication asks not only whether a message was delivered, but who interpreted it, under what conditions, what action became possible, and what evidence supports the claimed effect.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. distinguish interpersonal, community, mass-media and digital channels;
  2. choose channels according to communication objective rather than fashion;
  3. explain reach, frequency, exposure and engagement;
  4. assess channel credibility, accessibility and cost;
  5. distinguish owned, earned, paid and shared media;
  6. design an integrated channel mix;
  7. select and prepare credible messengers and spokespersons;
  8. recognise channel-specific risks including exclusion and message distortion;
  9. develop a basic media plan and monitoring framework.

ORIENT · “Put it on social media” is not a strategy

A programme wants to increase childhood immunisation in remote communities. The communications team proposes TikTok because it is “where everyone is.” Data show that many caregivers use basic phones, local radio has high reach, village health teams are trusted, and missed vaccination is strongly associated with clinic distance and uncertainty about outreach dates.

Channel choice should follow audience and objective evidence—not team preference.

LEARN 1 · Channels perform different communication jobs

Interpersonal channels are strong for dialogue and personalised questions. Community channels can support norms, local problem solving and trust. Mass media provide broad reach. Digital channels can offer speed, targeting and interaction. Print can provide durable reference information. No channel dominates every objective.

LEARN 2 · Choose channels against explicit criteria

Consider reach, frequency, credibility, accessibility, cost, speed, interactivity, privacy, message complexity, language and proximity to the decision point. A long counselling conversation can explain complex trade-offs; a roadside billboard cannot.

LEARN 3 · Reach is not frequency and neither is impact

Reach is the number or proportion exposed. Frequency is how often they are exposed. Engagement indicates some interaction. None of these alone proves comprehension, behaviour change or health impact.

LEARN 4 · Owned, earned, paid and shared media

Owned channels are controlled by the organisation, such as a website or SMS list. Earned media is attention obtained through journalism or public interest. Paid media purchases placement. Shared media circulates through social networks and communities. Each has different control, credibility and cost.

LEARN 5 · Integrated communication creates reinforcement

A radio announcement can create awareness, an SMS can provide date and location, a community health worker can answer questions, and a facility sign can confirm the service point. Integration should reduce contradiction and make the pathway to action easier.

LEARN 6 · Messengers are strategic choices

A messenger should have credibility with the intended audience, understand the content and be able to communicate respectfully. The most senior official is not automatically the most trusted messenger. Health workers, peers, religious leaders, teachers, survivors or local experts may be more appropriate for particular objectives.

LEARN 7 · Media relations require preparation

Spokespersons should know key messages, likely questions, uncertainty boundaries and what cannot yet be answered. Bridges back to core points should not become evasive. Correct errors quickly and maintain consistency across spokespeople.

LEARN 8 · Channel choice can create inequity

Digital-only strategies can exclude people without smartphones, data, literacy or privacy. Public loudspeakers can expose sensitive information. Printed materials may fail for visually impaired audiences. Every channel plan should include an exclusion analysis.

LEARN 9 · Timing is part of channel strategy

A reminder after the clinic has closed is useless. Communication should reach people before and during the decision window. Frequency should be sufficient for recall without creating fatigue.

LEARN 10 · Monitor delivery and decision outcomes

Track whether content was delivered, who was reached, who understood, whether intended actions became easier and whether some groups were missed. Channel metrics are implementation evidence, not substitutes for programme outcomes.

DEEPEN · Channels are delivery systems, not strategy by themselves

“Use radio and social media” is not a communication strategy. Channel choice should follow the audience, objective, trust environment, required interaction, urgency, frequency and cost.

Channel characteristics

Mass media can provide broad reach quickly but may offer limited personal clarification. Community and interpersonal channels can support dialogue and trust but require trained people and time. Digital channels allow rapid targeting and feedback yet can exclude people without devices, data, literacy or safe private access.

Owned, earned, paid and community channels

  • Owned: programme websites, SMS lists, clinic screens and official social accounts.
  • Earned: news coverage, interviews and organic discussion not directly purchased.
  • Paid: advertisements, sponsored posts and purchased airtime.
  • Community: local leaders, health workers, peer networks, faith organisations and community gatherings.

A resilient strategy usually combines channels so that one channel creates awareness while another supports questions, trust or action.

Reach, frequency and timing

Reach asks who encountered the communication. Frequency asks how often. Repetition can support recall, but excessive exposure can produce fatigue. Timing should match the moment when the audience can act: a reminder sent after the clinic has closed is technically delivered but operationally useless.

Messenger-channel fit

The most trusted messenger may not control the highest-reach channel. A national expert can explain technical evidence, while a local health worker can address practical concerns and a peer can demonstrate social feasibility. Coordination matters so these voices do not contradict one another.

Media relations

Journalists need timely, clear and verifiable information. Prepare spokespeople to state the main message, acknowledge uncertainty, avoid speculation and correct errors without becoming defensive. Media monitoring should identify both reach and emerging misunderstanding.

Low-bandwidth design

For many public-health contexts, communication plans should assume intermittent data, shared phones and basic handsets. SMS, radio, printable materials and community networks remain important. Digital sophistication is not a substitute for accessibility.

What could fool us?

High impressions do not prove that the intended audience saw or trusted a message. A popular channel may be inappropriate for a sensitive topic. Channel analytics often describe users of the platform rather than the target population. Always interpret channel metrics against the communication objective.

FIELD LAB · Give every channel a job

For a cervical-screening campaign, a channel mix might look like this:

Channel Primary job Why it fits Key indicator
radio awareness and normalisation broad local reach aided exposure and message recall
community health worker questions and trust interpersonal clarification questions resolved / referrals made
SMS reminder and logistics timely individual prompt delivery and appointment completion
clinic signage point-of-decision instruction reaches people already near service correct navigation / same-day uptake
social media shareable updates and dialogue useful for connected segments comprehension and referral clicks

The same message should not simply be copied into every channel. Radio can tell a short story; SMS needs concise logistics; a health worker can respond interactively.

Reach-frequency decision

A channel plan should state the intended audience, estimated reach, repetition schedule and decision timing. Repetition is valuable when it supports memory or confidence, but excessive frequency can create fatigue. Monitor both exposure and negative feedback.

Media briefing drill

Prepare a spokesperson to answer three questions in 30 seconds each: What is the problem? What should the public do? What remains uncertain? If the spokesperson cannot answer without jargon or speculation, the campaign is not ready for earned media.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A district wants to increase uptake of scheduled hypertension screening days among adults over 40. Urban residents commonly use WhatsApp and radio; rural residents rely more on radio, churches, markets and village health teams. Screening is offered monthly at fixed sites. Men are less likely to attend during working hours.

  1. State the communication objective.
  2. Compare at least five potential channels using reach, trust, timing, interactivity and cost.
  3. Design an integrated urban and rural media mix.
  4. Choose appropriate messengers.
  5. Explain how the strategy changes if confidentiality becomes a concern.
  6. Identify one channel that looks attractive but is poorly matched to the objective.
  7. Specify reach, frequency and action indicators.
  8. Identify one service change that should accompany communication.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. Why can the channel with the largest audience still be the wrong choice?
  2. What is the difference between reach and frequency?
  3. Give one example of earned media.
  4. Why might a peer messenger outperform a senior official?
  5. What is one equity risk of a digital-only strategy?
  6. Why should media metrics not be reported as behaviour-change outcomes?

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.

Pilot Mastery Quiz: 10 objective questions randomly sampled from a curated Week 7 bank of 30 items.

Initial time limit: 12 minutes. Mode: one question at a time; randomised item selection; answer options shuffled; limited attempts; feedback released according to course or institutional settings.

Assessment principle
Knowing the material should be the fastest strategy. Timing is calibrated to reward prepared reading and reasoning, not to replace sound question design or approved accessibility accommodations.
