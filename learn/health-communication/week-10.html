LIPPS LEARN · HEALTH COMMUNICATION · WEEK 10

Misinformation, Disinformation and Infodemic Management

The goal is not to win arguments online. It is to reduce the health harm created by a polluted information environment.

Infodemic Misinformation Social listening Resilience

This week in one sentence

Identify harmful information problems, prioritise them by likely health impact and respond through listening, accurate communication, trusted messengers, community engagement and resilience-building.

The LIPPS lens
Good health communication asks not only whether a message was delivered, but who interpreted it, under what conditions, what action became possible, and what evidence supports the claimed effect.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. distinguish misinformation, disinformation, rumours and infodemics;
  2. explain why information voids and uncertainty create fertile conditions for harmful narratives;
  3. use social listening to identify questions and emerging narratives;
  4. prioritise misinformation responses according to reach, harm, vulnerability and actionability;
  5. choose when to correct publicly and when not to amplify a low-reach claim;
  6. write corrections that lead with useful facts and concrete actions;
  7. explain prebunking and resilience-building approaches;
  8. integrate community engagement with digital responses;
  9. measure infodemic-response outputs and outcomes;
  10. recognise ethical risks in monitoring information environments.

ORIENT · The rumour is false—but should you repeat it?

A voice note claims that a new vaccine causes infertility. It has circulated in several closed messaging groups and a local radio presenter mentions it on air. A health agency drafts a poster with the rumour in huge red letters: “THE VACCINE DOES NOT CAUSE INFERTILITY.” Before publishing, the team should ask whether the correction will reduce harm or inadvertently spread the claim to people who had never heard it.

LEARN 1 · Define the information problem carefully

Misinformation is false or misleading information shared without necessarily intending harm. Disinformation involves deliberate creation or spread of false or misleading information for strategic purposes. A rumour is unverified information circulating within a community. An infodemic is an overabundance of information—including false or misleading information—that creates confusion and can undermine health action.

LEARN 2 · Infodemics are not only about false facts

People can be overwhelmed by contradictory guidance, outdated information, technical complexity, scams and true information stripped of context. Management therefore requires improving the overall information environment, not merely fact-checking false posts.

LEARN 3 · Information voids are a warning signal

When people have urgent unanswered questions, others fill the gap. Monitor what people are asking: eligibility, safety, side effects, access, cost, cause, responsibility and uncertainty. Fast, clear answers to genuine questions can prevent harmful narratives from gaining traction.

LEARN 4 · Social listening should produce actionable insight

Collect recurring questions and narratives, assess where they circulate, identify affected audiences, estimate likely harm and determine what response is possible. WHO frames infodemic management around listening, promoting understanding of risk and expert advice, building resilience to misinformation, and engaging communities for positive action.

LEARN 5 · Not every false claim deserves a mass response

Prioritise according to potential health harm, reach, velocity, credibility of the source, vulnerability of the audience and whether the claim changes behaviour. Publicly rebutting an obscure claim can amplify it.

LEARN 6 · Corrections should make the useful truth easy to remember

Lead with the accurate information and the action people need. Briefly identify the false claim only when necessary for recognition, explain why it is wrong using clear evidence, and repeat the accurate conclusion. Avoid ridicule; it can harden identity-based resistance and damage relationships.

LEARN 7 · Prebunking builds resilience before exposure

Prebunking or inoculation approaches warn people about common manipulation techniques or likely misleading narratives before they encounter them. The aim is not to predict every rumour, but to strengthen recognition of unreliable tactics and encourage verification habits.

LEARN 8 · Trusted messengers and community dialogue matter

A technically correct correction from a distrusted institution may fail. Work with health workers, community leaders, journalists and other credible intermediaries while ensuring they understand the evidence and are not used merely as endorsement props.

LEARN 9 · Measure beyond number of corrections issued

Possible indicators include time from detection to response, unanswered information needs, reach of accurate content among affected audiences, changes in belief certainty, intended protective action, trust, and whether harmful narratives decline or persist. Attribution is difficult because information environments are dynamic.

LEARN 10 · Ethics and privacy still apply

Social listening should minimise collection of identifiable information, avoid targeting individuals for punishment, and distinguish public-interest analysis from intrusive surveillance. Vulnerable groups should not be exposed or stigmatised through screenshots or examples.

DEEPEN · Manage information risk, not every incorrect sentence

WHO describes an infodemic as an overabundance of information, including false or misleading information, that can create confusion, mistrust and harmful behaviour. Infodemic management is therefore broader than fact-checking individual posts.

Distinguish the information problem

Misinformation is false or inaccurate information shared without necessarily intending harm. Disinformation is deliberately false or manipulated information intended to deceive. Harmful communication may also involve technically true information used out of context. The response depends on reach, credibility, audience vulnerability and likely health impact—not only truth status.

Listen before correcting

Social listening, community feedback, hotline questions and frontline-worker reports help identify recurring concerns and information voids. Code concerns by topic, audience, location, channel and potential consequence. Protect privacy and avoid storing unnecessary personal identifiers.

Prioritise

Not every rumour deserves amplification. A practical matrix considers reach, growth, credibility, behavioural consequence and affected population. A false claim seen by ten people may need quiet correction; a rapidly growing claim that discourages emergency care may require immediate coordinated response.

Prebunking and debunking

Prebunking anticipates predictable misleading tactics or claims before they spread widely. Debunking corrects a claim after exposure. Corrections should lead with accurate information, explain the problem clearly, avoid repeating sensational falsehoods unnecessarily and give people a useful action.

Fill information voids

Silence can be an intervention failure. When authorities do not explain an adverse event, service interruption or policy change, alternative explanations flourish. Rapid, transparent communication reduces the space for speculation.

Trusted messengers and resilience

People judge information partly through relationships. Work with trusted local messengers, but do not use trust as a shortcut for poor evidence. Long-term resilience also requires media and health literacy, community dialogue and institutions that respond to legitimate concerns.

What could fool us?

Online volume is not the same as population belief. Bots and highly active users can distort apparent prevalence. Removing a post can move the claim into less visible channels. Evaluate whether the response changes understanding, confidence or harmful behaviour—not only whether the content disappears.

FIELD LAB · Triage an information signal

Use a simple priority score rather than reacting equally to every rumour.

Dimension Low priority High priority
reach isolated rapidly expanding
credibility implausible source trusted or authority-like source
health consequence little behavioural effect delays care or promotes dangerous action
vulnerability low-risk audience people facing immediate health risk
persistence fading recurring across channels

A high-priority signal should trigger rapid verification, message preparation, messenger selection and monitoring. A low-priority signal may be best handled quietly through targeted clarification to avoid amplification.

Correction structure

Fact first → brief explanation → clear action → trusted source.

Example: “The measles vaccine cannot cause measles infection. It uses a weakened virus that trains the immune system. Children who are due for vaccination should attend the listed clinics this week. Updated clinic information is available from…”

The exact wording must fit the local evidence and audience. The structure keeps accurate information more prominent than the false claim.

Listening dashboard

Track topic, question/claim, source/channel, audience, location, trend, potential harm, response owner and status. Avoid retaining personal identifiers unless genuinely necessary.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

During an immunisation campaign, rumours circulate that the vaccine is part of a fertility-control programme. Some versions are low reach; another is repeated by a widely followed local personality. Health workers report that caregivers are asking whether the vaccine contains hormones.

  1. Classify the information problems: rumour, misinformation, disinformation or unknown intent.
  2. Identify the information void.
  3. Create a prioritisation matrix using reach, harm, vulnerability and actionability.
  4. Decide which claim requires public correction and which should be monitored.
  5. Draft a short correction that begins with accurate information and a practical action.
  6. Select trusted messengers and explain why.
  7. Propose one prebunking/resilience activity.
  8. Specify indicators for the next two weeks.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. Why is misinformation different from disinformation?
  2. What makes an infodemic broader than a list of false claims?
  3. Why can publicly rebutting a low-reach rumour be harmful?
  4. Name WHO’s four broad infodemic-management activities.
  5. What is an information void?
  6. What ethical concern arises when social listening captures identifiable posts from vulnerable individuals?

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.
