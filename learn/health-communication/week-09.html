LIPPS LEARN · HEALTH COMMUNICATION · WEEK 09

Risk and Crisis Communication

In an emergency, people need useful information before uncertainty disappears.

RCCE Uncertainty Trust Emergency

This week in one sentence

Communicate rapidly and transparently during health threats while acknowledging uncertainty, supporting protective action, listening to affected communities and adapting as evidence changes.

The LIPPS lens
Good health communication asks not only whether a message was delivered, but who interpreted it, under what conditions, what action became possible, and what evidence supports the claimed effect.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. define emergency risk communication and distinguish it from routine health promotion;
  2. explain why timeliness and trust are operational assets during crises;
  3. communicate what is known, unknown and being done to reduce uncertainty;
  4. identify factors influencing risk perception;
  5. develop concise action-oriented emergency messages;
  6. coordinate messengers and information across response partners;
  7. integrate community engagement and feedback into emergency communication;
  8. manage corrections and changing guidance without pretending certainty;
  9. design a basic communication response across preparedness, initial response and recovery phases.

ORIENT · Waiting for perfect information

Several patients develop severe unexplained haemorrhagic illness. Laboratory confirmation is pending. Officials delay public communication because they fear being wrong. Meanwhile, rumours spread that the hospital is hiding deaths and people begin avoiding care.

In emergencies, silence also communicates. The question is not whether uncertainty exists; it is how to communicate responsibly while uncertainty exists.

LEARN 1 · Risk communication is real-time exchange for protective decisions

WHO defines risk communication as the real-time exchange of information, advice and opinions between experts or officials and people facing a hazard. The purpose is to enable informed decisions that reduce harm. It includes media work, digital communication and community engagement.

LEARN 2 · Speed and accuracy are not opposites

Communicate early with what is verified, clearly distinguish preliminary information, and update as evidence changes. Delaying until every detail is certain creates information voids and can allow rumours to define the situation.

LEARN 3 · Say what is known, unknown and being done

A useful uncertainty structure is: What we know now → what we do not yet know → what people should do now → what authorities are doing to learn more → when the next update will come. This preserves usefulness without false certainty.

LEARN 4 · Risk perception is not determined by probability alone

People may perceive greater risk when hazards are unfamiliar, involuntary, catastrophic, invisible, unfair or associated with low institutional trust. Communicators should understand these perceptions rather than dismiss them as irrational.

LEARN 5 · Action messages should be feasible and prioritised

During a crisis, cognitive load is high. Prioritise a small number of protective actions, explain who should act, where and when, and update instructions as the situation changes. Avoid flooding audiences with every technical detail at once.

LEARN 6 · Empathy and respect support credibility

Acknowledge fear, loss, inconvenience and uncertainty. Empathy does not mean exaggerating emotion; it signals that public concerns are understood. Dismissive language can undermine technically accurate guidance.

LEARN 7 · Coordination prevents contradiction

Response agencies should align core facts, terminology, action recommendations and update schedules. Differences in institutional roles can be explained, but preventable contradictions rapidly damage confidence.

LEARN 8 · Community engagement is part of emergency communication

WHO’s current RCCE approach emphasises community-centred emergency programmes. Listening mechanisms should identify questions, rumours, barriers and unintended effects of response measures, and those insights should reach operational teams.

LEARN 9 · Changing guidance should be explained, not hidden

When evidence changes, explain what changed and why. A transparent revision can strengthen credibility; pretending that earlier guidance never existed invites distrust.

LEARN 10 · Prepare before the emergency

Build spokesperson capacity, audience maps, channel agreements, translation workflows, social-listening systems, approval pathways and simulation exercises before a crisis. Preparedness reduces the delay between signal and useful communication.

DEEPEN · Communicating when evidence is incomplete

Risk communication becomes most important when information is changing, consequences matter and people are anxious. Waiting for perfect certainty can create an information vacuum that others fill.

CDC’s Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication approach emphasises communication that is early, accurate, credible, empathetic, action-oriented and respectful. WHO’s RCCE framework similarly places communities at the centre of preparedness and response.

The first announcement

A strong first announcement should state what happened, what is known, what is not yet known, what authorities are doing, what people should do now, and when more information will be provided. Do not create false reassurance by turning uncertainty into certainty.

Risk is interpreted socially

People judge risk through more than probability. Dread, unfamiliarity, fairness, control, trust and whether children are affected can change perceived risk. A communication team should understand these concerns rather than dismissing them as irrational.

Empathy before explanation

Acknowledging fear, loss or disruption creates space for technical information. Empathy does not mean exaggerating danger or agreeing with false claims. It signals that the response understands what people are experiencing.

Spokesperson discipline

Spokespeople should know what they can confirm, what they cannot confirm and who has authority to speak. Speculation becomes especially damaging when different agencies contradict one another. Coordination should reduce unnecessary inconsistency without suppressing legitimate uncertainty.

Update rhythm

Commit to the next update even when no major new finding is expected. “We will update at 4 p.m.” is more credible than silence. Correct previous information openly when evidence changes and explain why the recommendation changed.

Community feedback during crisis

Hotlines, local leaders, health workers and digital monitoring can reveal emerging questions and rumours. Feedback must be analysed quickly enough to change communication or operations.

What could fool us?

A drop in public questions may reflect reassurance, but it may also mean people stopped trusting official channels. High media coverage can coexist with low comprehension. Crisis evaluation should therefore triangulate reach, understanding, trust, action and operational data.

FIELD LAB · Draft the first public statement

A first statement can follow this order:

  1. Acknowledge: recognise what people are experiencing.
  2. State what is known: use only verified information and a clear cut-off time.
  3. State what is not known: identify the most important uncertainties.
  4. Give action: tell people what they can do now.
  5. Describe the response: explain what authorities are doing.
  6. Commit to an update: give a time or trigger for the next communication.

Example opening: “We are investigating a cluster of acute watery diarrhoea reported since yesterday in three villages. Samples are being tested. People in the affected area should use treated or boiled water, wash hands with soap, and seek care immediately for severe diarrhoea or signs of dehydration. We will provide the next update at 4 p.m. today.”

The statement avoids declaring a cause before evidence is available while still enabling protective action.

Update log

Maintain a table of time, new evidence, message change, reason for change, channels used and unresolved questions. This prevents contradictory statements and makes later evaluation possible.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A district reports suspected cholera after severe flooding. Laboratory confirmation will take 24 hours. Several water points are damaged, social media claims that chlorine is poisonous, and residents say previous emergency messages arrived only in English.

  1. Draft the first public statement using known/unknown/action/update structure.
  2. Select three immediate protective actions and explain why they are prioritised.
  3. Identify audiences requiring different operational information.
  4. Choose messengers and channels for the first 12 hours.
  5. Design a community-listening mechanism.
  6. Plan how to respond to the chlorine concern without amplifying it unnecessarily.
  7. State what you would communicate when laboratory confirmation arrives.
  8. Identify one operational response problem that communication must escalate.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. Why can silence worsen a crisis even when facts are uncertain?
  2. What four elements should accompany communication of uncertainty?
  3. Name two factors that can increase perceived risk beyond statistical probability.
  4. Why should changing guidance be explained?
  5. How does community engagement improve risk communication?
  6. What should be prepared before an emergency rather than improvised during it?

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.
