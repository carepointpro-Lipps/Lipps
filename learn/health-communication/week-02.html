LIPPS LEARN · HEALTH COMMUNICATION · WEEK 02

Communication and Behaviour-Change Theory

Theory is useful when it helps you diagnose why behaviour is happening—not when it is added after the campaign is already designed.

Theory Behaviour COM-B Social context

This week in one sentence

Use behavioural and communication theory to diagnose why a health behaviour occurs and to select plausible communication and non-communication responses.

The LIPPS lens
Good health communication asks not only whether a message was delivered, but who interpreted it, under what conditions, what action became possible, and what evidence supports the claimed effect.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. explain why theory can improve communication planning;
  2. distinguish determinants of behaviour from messages intended to influence them;
  3. apply key constructs from the Health Belief Model, Theory of Planned Behaviour and Social Cognitive Theory;
  4. use the social-ecological perspective to distinguish individual, interpersonal, community and structural influences;
  5. apply COM-B as a practical diagnostic framework for capability, opportunity and motivation;
  6. explain diffusion of innovations concepts including relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability;
  7. choose a theory because it fits the problem rather than because it is familiar;
  8. translate theoretical constructs into measurable communication objectives.

ORIENT · “People know—but they still do not do it”

A community survey finds that almost everyone knows mosquito nets reduce malaria, yet consistent net use remains low. A campaign team proposes another awareness campaign. Before approving it, ask: Is knowledge actually the limiting determinant? Are nets available? Are they uncomfortable in hot weather? Do households reserve them for children? Are there beliefs about chemicals? Do sleeping arrangements make hanging difficult?

Theory helps organise these explanations and prevents communication from treating every behaviour problem as an information deficit.

LEARN 1 · Theory is a map, not a magic formula

Behavioural theories organise plausible determinants and relationships. They help planners decide what to measure, what mechanism to target and what change would count as progress. A theory should be judged by its usefulness for the specific problem, not by how famous it is.

LEARN 2 · Health Belief Model: perceived threat and action

The Health Belief Model focuses on perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action and self-efficacy. It can be useful when a person must decide whether a preventive or care-seeking action is worthwhile. Its weakness is that it can understate social, structural and environmental constraints if used alone.

LEARN 3 · Theory of Planned Behaviour: intention is socially situated

The Theory of Planned Behaviour links attitudes toward the behaviour, perceived social norms and perceived behavioural control to behavioural intention. It reminds communicators that a person may personally favour an action but still avoid it because important others disapprove or because they believe they cannot perform it.

LEARN 4 · Social Cognitive Theory: people learn in social environments

Social Cognitive Theory emphasises reciprocal interaction among people, behaviour and environment. Important concepts include self-efficacy, observational learning, outcome expectations, goals and reinforcement. Demonstration and credible modelling can therefore matter as much as factual instruction.

LEARN 5 · Social-ecological thinking: behaviour has levels

A social-ecological lens examines influences at individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy/system levels. Low vaccination uptake, for example, may reflect individual concerns, household decision-making, provider behaviour, clinic waiting time and policy or supply constraints simultaneously.

LEARN 6 · COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation

COM-B provides a practical diagnostic: behaviour occurs when people have sufficient capability, opportunity and motivation. Capability may be physical or psychological; opportunity may be social or physical; motivation may be reflective or automatic. Communication can influence some of these determinants, but it cannot create vaccine stock or shorten a 30-kilometre journey.

LEARN 7 · Diffusion of innovations: adoption is not uniform

New practices spread differently across social systems. Adoption is affected by perceived relative advantage, compatibility with existing values and routines, complexity, trialability and observability. Opinion leaders and peer networks can influence diffusion, but “early adopter” labels should not become stereotypes.

LEARN 8 · Choose the mechanism before the message

If the problem is low self-efficacy, demonstrate the behaviour and build confidence. If social norms are the barrier, private information alone may do little. If physical opportunity is absent, communication should support service redesign rather than disguise an access problem as a knowledge problem.

LEARN 9 · From theory to measurable objective

A theoretical construct becomes useful when it can be translated into something observable. “Improve attitudes” is vague. “Increase the proportion of eligible adults who believe hypertension screening is worthwhile despite feeling healthy” is more specific and measurable.

DEEPEN · From theory to an intervention mechanism

A theory becomes useful only when it changes what you measure, design or decide. LIPPS therefore uses a four-step chain:

determinant → mechanism → intervention component → indicator.

If women miss antenatal contacts because they believe later visits add little value, the determinant is not simply “poor knowledge”. A plausible mechanism is low perceived benefit. Communication might therefore use a credible explanation of what later contacts detect that the first visit cannot. An intermediate indicator could be the proportion of women who correctly identify the purpose of later contacts. By contrast, if women intend to attend but cannot afford transport, the dominant problem is physical opportunity. A stronger poster does not create transport.

Diagnostic question Useful theoretical lens Example response
Does the person believe the threat applies to them? Health Belief Model clarify susceptibility without exaggerating fear
Do important others approve of the behaviour? Theory of Planned Behaviour address norms and influential referents
Does the person feel able to perform the behaviour? Self-efficacy / perceived control demonstration, rehearsal, skills and problem-solving
Is behaviour constrained by resources or the environment? COM-B / social-ecological thinking change opportunity, services or policy as well as communication
Will people see the innovation as useful and compatible? Diffusion of innovations demonstrate relative advantage and fit with routines

Theory fit, not theory shopping

A common academic error is to mention several theories because a proposal appears more sophisticated when it has many boxes and arrows. That is theory decoration. A defensible choice begins with evidence about the behaviour, identifies the most plausible determinants, and then selects the smallest theoretical toolkit that helps explain or change them.

Worked example · Hypertension screening

Suppose adults in an urban community agree that hypertension is serious, but many say, “I feel fine, so I do not need screening.” The important barrier is not severity. It is the belief that absence of symptoms implies absence of risk. A useful communication objective might be: increase the proportion of adults aged 30–60 who understand that hypertension may be asymptomatic and who can identify a convenient screening point. If screening sites are open only during working hours, however, the programme must also address opportunity.

What could fool us?

A determinant measured in a cross-sectional survey may be associated with behaviour without being its cause. People who already attend services may subsequently report higher self-efficacy or more favourable attitudes. Social-desirability bias can make stated intentions look stronger than real-world behaviour. Theory should organize inquiry, not manufacture causality from weak data.

FIELD LAB · Diagnose before you communicate

Use this rapid worksheet before approving a behaviour-change campaign.

Step Question ANC example
Behaviour What exactly should change? complete the recommended antenatal contacts
Observed gap What is happening now? many women attend once but do not return
Candidate determinant What evidence suggests why? later visits seen as unnecessary; transport and queues also matter
Theory construct Which construct helps organise that determinant? perceived benefit, subjective norm, opportunity
Communication mechanism What could communication plausibly change? understanding of the purpose of later visits; partner support
Non-communication action What must the service/system change? appointment flow, waiting time, transport barriers
Intermediate indicator What should change before behaviour? correct understanding, confidence, partner support
Behaviour indicator What final action is observed? attendance at subsequent contacts

The purpose of the table is not to force every programme into one theory. It is to make the causal story visible enough to challenge. If the proposed message has no plausible path to the measured outcome, the strategy needs revision.

Mini worked example

A district survey finds that 88% of women know antenatal care is recommended, 73% intend to return, but only 41% complete later contacts. Interviews show long queues and partner-controlled transport money. A knowledge campaign is therefore a weak first response. The evidence points toward opportunity and household decision-making. Communication may still help by preparing partners and clarifying appointment value, but service redesign is central.

Reflection

Before naming a theory, write one sentence beginning: “The behaviour is not occurring because…” If that sentence is vague, theory selection is premature.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A district has low completion of four or more antenatal contacts. Interviews suggest that many women intend to attend but face transport costs, partners sometimes control household money, clinic queues are long, and some women believe later visits are unnecessary if the first visit found no problem.

  1. Use COM-B to classify the main determinants.
  2. Apply at least three Health Belief Model constructs.
  3. Identify one relevant subjective norm and one perceived-control issue.
  4. Use a social-ecological lens to identify influences at three levels.
  5. State which problems communication could plausibly address and which require service or policy action.
  6. Write two theory-linked communication objectives.
  7. Propose one message strategy and one environmental/service strategy.
  8. Explain why another generic “ANC is important” campaign could fail.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. Why is awareness sometimes a poor target for behaviour-change communication?
  2. Give an example of perceived barrier and self-efficacy for the same health behaviour.
  3. How does perceived behavioural control differ from intention?
  4. What does COM-B add when a programme is over-focused on knowledge?
  5. Why might social modelling influence behaviour?
  6. Which diffusion attribute is involved when people reject an innovation because it conflicts with established practice?

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.
