LIPPS LEARN · HEALTH COMMUNICATION · WEEK 06

Message Development and Pre-testing

The first draft is a hypothesis. Pre-testing is how you learn whether the audience interprets it the way you intended.

Message design Plain language Pre-test Revision

This week in one sentence

Develop clear, actionable health messages from defined objectives and improve them through systematic pre-testing with intended audiences.

The LIPPS lens
Good health communication asks not only whether a message was delivered, but who interpreted it, under what conditions, what action became possible, and what evidence supports the claimed effect.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. translate a communication objective into a message strategy;
  2. distinguish core message, supporting evidence and call to action;
  3. apply clarity, relevance, credibility and actionability criteria;
  4. use framing carefully without distorting evidence;
  5. write plain-language health messages;
  6. identify visual and numerical communication risks;
  7. design a message pre-test;
  8. distinguish comprehension, relevance, acceptability, credibility and intended action;
  9. revise messages using evidence rather than preference;
  10. recognise when adaptation is needed instead of direct translation.

ORIENT · “Scientifically perfect” and communicatively useless

A vaccination poster reads: “Eligible individuals are advised to receive the bivalent booster to optimise immunogenicity and mitigate severe morbidity.” The technical team approves it because every word is accurate. Community pre-testing shows that many people do not know whether they are “eligible,” what “bivalent” means, or where to go.

Accuracy is necessary. It is not sufficient.

LEARN 1 · Start from an objective and one priority action

A message should serve a defined communication objective. Decide what the audience needs to understand or do first. Trying to place every fact on one poster usually produces information overload.

LEARN 2 · Use a message hierarchy

A practical hierarchy is core message → supporting reason/evidence → specific action → where/when/how → source. The core message should survive even if a person reads or hears only the first few seconds.

LEARN 3 · Actionability needs operational detail

“Seek care early” is weak if people do not know what “early” means, which symptoms matter, where to go, when services are open or whether the service costs money. Communication should remove avoidable uncertainty around the desired action.

LEARN 4 · Framing changes perception

Gain frames emphasise benefits of action; loss frames emphasise costs of inaction. Both can be legitimate, but fear or loss framing should not exaggerate risk or shame audiences. Strong emotion without efficacy can produce avoidance rather than action.

LEARN 5 · Plain language preserves meaning

Use familiar terms, active voice, concrete verbs, short sentences and logical order. Put the important action first. Replace institutional language with audience language, but do not oversimplify to the point of inaccuracy.

LEARN 6 · Numbers and visuals can mislead unintentionally

Truncated axes, distorted icons, inconsistent denominators, tiny footnotes and decorative graphics can create false impressions. Visual design should make the intended comparison easier, not more dramatic.

LEARN 7 · Pre-testing asks whether the message works as intended

Key domains include comprehension, attention, relevance, acceptability, credibility, cultural fit, actionability and unintended interpretations. Recall can be useful but should not be confused with behaviour change.

LEARN 8 · Use methods matched to the question

Cognitive interviews can explore how people interpret wording. Focus groups can reveal shared reactions and social meaning. Intercept tests can quickly compare alternatives. Digital A/B tests can compare observable engagement, but clicks alone do not prove understanding or health impact.

LEARN 9 · Pre-test with intended users, not only colleagues

Experts know too much about the topic to reliably predict what a first-time reader will understand. A message that seems obvious in the programme office may be ambiguous in the real setting.

LEARN 10 · Revision is part of the design

Document what changed and why. If pre-testing reveals a service barrier rather than a message problem, change the service or expectation rather than trying to “wordsmith” around it.

DEEPEN · Build the message from the decision backwards

A message should begin with the action or decision it must support. Before writing copy, specify the audience, the desired action, the main barrier, the benefit that matters to the audience and the evidence that must be conveyed.

One main message does not mean one sentence

People can process supporting details, but the material should make the main point unmistakable. The CDC Clear Communication Index asks whether the main message and call to action are obvious and whether numbers are explained in ways people can use. A technically complete brochure that hides the key action on page four is poorly designed.

Plain language is precision

Plain language removes unnecessary complexity without removing scientific meaning. Replace jargon, shorten sentences, use familiar words, define unavoidable technical terms and make instructions concrete. “Present for clinical evaluation if symptoms persist” is less actionable than “Return to the clinic if the fever is still present after two days.”

Communicating numbers

Use consistent denominators, meaningful frequencies and absolute risks when possible. “Risk doubled” can sound dramatic even when risk changed from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000. Visuals should clarify rather than decorate.

Pre-testing is diagnosis, not voting

Ask intended users what they think the message means, what action they believe is expected, what is confusing, what feels credible and whether anything is offensive or unrealistic. Avoid asking only, “Do you like it?” A liked message can still be misunderstood.

Translation and adaptation

Literal translation can preserve words while losing meaning, tone or social acceptability. Back-translation is useful but insufficient. Test the adapted message with people who actually use the language in the intended setting.

Iteration

Pre-testing should change the product. Document the issue identified, the revision made and whether the revised version solved the problem. This creates an audit trail and prevents teams from treating pre-testing as ceremonial approval.

What could fool us?

People may explain a message correctly in an interview because the interviewer prompts them. Testing in a quiet office can overestimate comprehension under real-world conditions. A visually attractive message may score well with programme staff but fail among the intended audience. Test where possible under conditions that resemble actual use.

FIELD LAB · From dense message to usable instruction

Draft: “Eligible beneficiaries are advised to present at designated health facilities for hypertension screening and subsequent clinical evaluation where indicated.”

The sentence is technically understandable to professionals but forces the public to decode several ideas.

Reworked: “High blood pressure often has no symptoms. If you are 30 years or older, get your blood pressure checked at the nearest participating health centre this month. The check takes a few minutes.”

The revision makes the risk, audience, action, place and timing easier to identify. Whether it is actually effective still requires pre-testing.

Five pre-test probes

Ask intended users:

  1. What is the main point in your own words?
  2. Who is this for?
  3. What should the person do next?
  4. What is confusing, worrying or hard to believe?
  5. Is the action realistically possible for someone like you?

Record the answer before explaining the intended meaning. Once the interviewer teaches the message, the pre-test is contaminated.

Revision log

Maintain a simple table with version, problem identified, evidence source, revision made and result of re-test. This becomes part of the quality record and is especially useful when multiple partners approve the same material.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A family-planning programme drafts: “Modern contraceptive methods are safe, effective and available. Visit your nearest facility today.” Formative research shows that many women worry about side effects, some need discreet access, adolescents fear judgement from providers, and several methods are intermittently out of stock.

  1. Identify the communication objective.
  2. Write a core message and supporting information for one priority audience.
  3. Make the call to action specific.
  4. Identify one claim that needs evidence qualification.
  5. Design five pre-test questions covering different domains.
  6. Choose two pre-testing methods and justify them.
  7. Identify one problem that should trigger service change rather than message revision.
  8. Produce a revised message after hypothetical feedback that “safe” was interpreted as “no side effects.”

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. What is the difference between a core message and supporting information?
  2. Why is “seek care early” often not actionable enough?
  3. Name four domains a message pre-test should examine.
  4. Why can expert review not replace audience pre-testing?
  5. What is a risk of fear appeals when efficacy is low?
  6. What does a high click-through rate fail to tell you?

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.

DEMONSTRATE · Week 6 mastery quiz

Pilot Mastery Quiz: 10 objective questions randomly sampled from a curated Week 6 bank of 30 items.

Initial time limit: 12 minutes. Mode: one question at a time; randomised item selection; answer options shuffled; limited attempts; feedback released according to course or institutional settings.

Assessment principle
Knowing the material should be the fastest strategy. Timing is calibrated to reward prepared reading and reasoning, not to replace sound question design or approved accessibility accommodations.
