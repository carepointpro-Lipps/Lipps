LIPPS LEARN · EPIDEMIOLOGY · WEEK 01

Epidemiology and Epidemiological Thinking

Before the formula, learn to see the population.

Epidemiology is not primarily a collection of statistical tests. It is a disciplined way of asking what is happening to health in a population, how often it is happening, to whom, where, when, why it may be happening, and what should be done with that knowledge.

Foundation Population thinking Applied reasoning Objective quiz

This week in one sentence

Define the health event, locate it in a population, put the number in context, compare what you observe with what you would expect, and use the result to inform action.

The LIPPS lens
A number is not yet evidence. A trend is not yet an explanation. A statistical association is not yet a cause. Epidemiological thinking begins by asking what else must be known before the observation can support a conclusion.

Learning outcomes

By the end of Week 1, you should be able to:

  1. define epidemiology and explain the meaning of distribution, determinants, specified populations, and application;
  2. distinguish the clinical view of an individual patient from the epidemiological view of a population;
  3. organise a public-health problem using what, who, where, when, and why/how;
  4. distinguish frequency from pattern, and explain why a denominator matters;
  5. distinguish introductory descriptive from analytic epidemiological reasoning;
  6. explain why valid comparison groups are central to analytic epidemiology;
  7. identify major historical contributions of Hippocrates, John Graunt, William Farr, and John Snow;
  8. describe major uses and core functions of epidemiology in public-health practice;
  9. explain the simple epidemiological sequence define → count → divide → compare → interpret → act; and
  10. convert a vague health concern into a focused epidemiological question.

ORIENT · A district says malaria has doubled

Consider this fictional teaching scenario.

A district health team reports 240 laboratory-confirmed malaria cases this month, compared with 120 last month. A message quickly circulates that malaria transmission has doubled.

It sounds straightforward. It is not.

Before accepting that conclusion, an epidemiologist would ask:

  • Did the number of people tested also change?
  • Did the case definition change?
  • Did more facilities report this month?
  • Did a new diagnostic test become available?
  • Are both months comparable in season and population size?
  • Are cases concentrated in particular places or groups?
  • Is the increase above what is normally expected at this time of year?

Your first decision

Which statement is the most defensible from the information given?

A. Malaria incidence doubled.
B. Malaria transmission doubled.
C. The reported number of confirmed cases doubled.
D. A malaria outbreak has been confirmed.

Best answer: C. The other statements require information that has not yet been supplied.

LEARN 1 · What is epidemiology?

A widely used public-health definition describes epidemiology as the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, together with the application of that study to the control of health problems.

The definition is short, but almost every word matters.

Study

Epidemiology is systematic inquiry. It depends on deliberate collection, analysis and interpretation of observations. Quantitative reasoning is important, but epidemiology also draws on biological, behavioural, social and other knowledge when explaining patterns.

Distribution

Distribution asks how health events are distributed in the population.

It has two ideas:

Frequency — How much disease or another health event is occurring?
Pattern — How is it distributed by person, place and time?

A count tells you how many events were observed. It does not, by itself, tell you how common the event is relative to the population from which the events arose.

Determinants

Determinants are factors that influence the occurrence of health-related events. Epidemiologists examine differences in exposures, behaviours, environments, demographic characteristics and other factors that may help explain why some groups experience more disease than others.

Health-related states or events

Modern epidemiology is not limited to infectious disease. Its methods can be applied to chronic disease, injury, disability, maternal and child health, occupational and environmental health, behaviours, service use and many other conditions affecting population health.

Specified populations

Epidemiology is explicitly population-based. We need to know which population the observations came from and which population our conclusion is intended to describe.

Application

Epidemiology is not complete when the analysis ends. In public-health practice, the knowledge generated should help guide prevention, control, planning, policy, evaluation or another appropriate action.

Do not memorise the definition as a sentence.
Be able to interrogate it. What was measured? In whom? How was occurrence distributed? What might explain it? What decision could the evidence inform?

LEARN 2 · The five questions behind epidemiological thinking

A useful way to organise a health event is the familiar five-W structure.

Question Epidemiological equivalent Example question
What? Health event / case definition What exactly counts as a malaria case?
Who? Person Which age, sex, occupation or risk groups are affected?
Where? Place Which villages, facilities or geographic areas are affected?
When? Time When did the increase begin, and is there a seasonal pattern?
Why / how? Determinants, causes, risk factors, transmission What exposures or conditions could explain the pattern?

The first four questions mainly help us characterise what we observe. The fifth moves us toward explaining it.

A useful discipline

When you encounter a health claim, delay the question “Which statistical test should I use?”

First ask:

  1. What is the health event?
  2. What is the population?
  3. What is the relevant time period?
  4. What is the denominator?
  5. What is the comparison?
  6. What alternative explanations could produce the observation?

Only then should the analytical method enter the discussion.

LEARN 3 · The epidemiologist’s patient is the population

Clinical medicine and epidemiology often examine the same disease from different levels.

Clinical question Epidemiological question
Does this patient have cholera? Is cholera occurrence higher than expected in this population?
What treatment does this patient need? What source or exposure could be driving cases?
What is this patient’s prognosis? Which groups have the highest risk, and how is risk changing?
What investigation should I order? What data and comparisons are needed to explain the population pattern?

The clinician’s immediate unit of concern is usually the individual. Epidemiology views individuals collectively to understand the health of the population.

The disciplines are complementary. Clinical observations can generate epidemiological signals, while epidemiological evidence can change clinical and public-health practice.

LIPPS checkpoint

A hospital reports 18 children with severe diarrhoea in one week. The hospital ordinarily sees 6–10 such cases per week.

The clinician asks: How should each child be treated?
The epidemiologist asks: Is this increase real, who is affected, what exposures are shared, and is anyone else at risk?

LEARN 4 · Frequency is not just a count

Suppose:

  • District A reports 200 cases.
  • District B reports 100 cases.

It is tempting to say District A has more disease.

But suppose District A has 1,000,000 residents and District B has 100,000.

The counts still matter for planning resources, but comparing population occurrence requires a denominator. This is the logic behind rates, risks and proportions that we will study formally in Week 2.

The foundational habit

Whenever you see a number, ask:

Out of how many?

Whenever you see a percentage, ask:

What exactly is in the numerator and denominator?

Evidence can mislead through denominator blindness.
A district can report more cases because it has more people, more testing, more complete reporting, or a different population structure. Week 1 teaches you to notice that problem; Week 2 teaches you how to quantify it.

LEARN 5 · Descriptive and analytic epidemiology

Descriptive epidemiology: what is happening?

Descriptive epidemiology characterises health events by time, place and person. It helps identify patterns and generate hypotheses.

Typical questions include:

  • When did cases increase?
  • Where are cases concentrated?
  • Which groups are most affected?
  • Is the pattern seasonal?

Analytic epidemiology: why might it be happening?

Analytic epidemiology goes further by comparing groups in order to examine whether an exposure or characteristic is associated with an outcome.

A critical idea is the comparison group.

If nearly every person with an illness ate a particular food, that observation may look persuasive. But if nearly everyone who remained well also ate that food, it becomes much less informative. The comparison gives the exposure history meaning.

Descriptive does not mean unimportant

Description can itself guide immediate action when a pattern is striking and delay could cause harm. More commonly, descriptive patterns generate hypotheses that are subsequently examined more rigorously.

We return to descriptive epidemiology in Week 4 and individual analytic study designs in Weeks 5–8. For now, understand the logic connecting pattern → hypothesis → comparison → interpretation.

LEARN 6 · Four people who changed epidemiological thinking

Hippocrates — look for natural explanations

Around 400 BCE, Hippocratic thinking challenged purely supernatural explanations of disease and considered environmental and host-related influences on illness.

LIPPS lesson: health patterns may have systematic explanations that can be investigated.

John Graunt — count population events

In 1662, John Graunt analysed mortality records and quantified patterns in births, deaths, infant mortality, sex differences, seasonal variation and urban-rural differences.

LIPPS lesson: routinely collected records can reveal population patterns when organised and compared.

William Farr — make population measurement systematic

Farr advanced the systematic collection, classification, analysis and reporting of mortality statistics and helped establish practices associated with vital statistics and surveillance.

LIPPS lesson: useful evidence requires systems that produce comparable information over time.

John Snow — description, comparison and action

During the 1854 London cholera investigations, Snow mapped where deaths occurred, investigated water sources, compared cholera mortality across populations receiving water from different companies, and used the findings to support public-health action.

The famous map matters, but the deeper lesson is the sequence of reasoning:

observe → describe → hypothesise → compare → interpret → act.

In one comparison reproduced in the CDC lesson, districts supplied only by Southwark and Vauxhall had a cholera death rate of 5.0 per 1,000, while districts supplied only by Lambeth had 0.9 per 1,000. This supported the contaminated-water hypothesis, while still leaving room to ask whether the populations differed in other relevant ways.

Quick calculation

Without yet worrying about the formal name of the measure, approximately how many times larger is 5.0 than 0.9?

5.0 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 5.6.

Week 3 will formalise this kind of comparison.

What made Snow’s work epidemiological was not simply drawing a map.
It was the combination of defined observations, population pattern, a plausible hypothesis, comparison, critical interpretation and action.

LEARN 7 · What is epidemiology used for?

The CDC lesson presents several broad uses that remain useful for organising public-health thinking.

Assess the health of a community

Epidemiological information helps determine what health problems exist, where they occur, which groups are at greater risk, which problems are increasing or declining, and whether services correspond to population need.

Inform individual decisions

Population evidence eventually influences personal choices and clinical recommendations through knowledge about risks and preventive interventions.

Complete the clinical picture

When a new or poorly understood condition emerges, aggregation of cases can help define its clinical spectrum, natural history and groups at risk.

Search for causes and prevent recurrence

Epidemiological studies can identify exposures and other factors that support prevention and control. Epidemiology may not, by itself, establish every element of biological causation, but it can generate evidence strong enough to support appropriate public-health action.

LEARN 8 · Core epidemiological functions in public-health practice

Epidemiology is not a single activity. The CDC framework identifies several interconnected functions.

Function What it means in practice Example
Surveillance Ongoing collection, analysis, interpretation and dissemination of health information for action Reviewing weekly disease reports and looking for unusual patterns
Field investigation Investigating cases, clusters or signals in their real-world setting Visiting affected communities and tracing likely exposures
Analytic studies Using structured comparisons to test explanations Comparing exposure histories of affected and unaffected groups
Evaluation Assessing whether programmes or systems are relevant, effective, efficient or achieving intended results Evaluating whether a screening programme reaches its target population
Linkages Working across disciplines, institutions and jurisdictions Epidemiologists working with clinicians, laboratories, environmental health teams and community leaders
Policy development Translating evidence and local understanding into recommendations or policy input Using evidence to recommend a prevention or control measure

A single problem may move through several functions: surveillance detects a signal, field investigation clarifies it, analytic work tests hypotheses, evaluation examines the response, linkages make implementation possible, and policy turns evidence into action.

LEARN 9 · Define → count → divide → compare

The CDC lesson reduces the basic epidemiological approach to three memorable operations: count, divide and compare. LIPPS places the decisions that must happen before and after those operations around them.

1. Define

Before counting cases, decide what qualifies as the health event. A case definition provides standard criteria for classification.

In outbreak work, a case definition may include clinical criteria and restrictions on time, place and person. If the case definition changes during surveillance, reported case numbers can change even when underlying disease occurrence does not.

2. Count

How many events occurred?

Counts are essential for workload, commodity and service planning. But they often cannot support fair population comparisons by themselves.

3. Divide

Relate events to an appropriate population denominator. Week 2 develops this formally.

4. Compare

Compare across populations, exposure groups, places or time periods. Ask what would normally be expected.

5. Interpret

Consider design, measurement, chance, bias, confounding, context and alternative explanations.

6. Act

Communicate what the evidence supports and use it appropriately for public-health decision making.

A LIPPS habit worth keeping for the entire course
When an analysis produces an impressive number, trace backwards: How was the event defined? Who entered the numerator? Who belonged in the denominator? What was the comparison? What else could produce the pattern?

APPLY · Turn a vague concern into an epidemiological question

Fictional scenario

A district health officer says:

“Teenage pregnancy is becoming a serious problem in our district.”

That is a concern, not yet an epidemiological question.

Step 1 — What?

What exactly will count as the outcome? Pregnancy among girls aged 15–19? Births among girls aged 15–19? First pregnancy? Current pregnancy? A routine-service indicator?

Step 2 — Who?

Which population are we describing? All girls aged 15–19 resident in the district? Girls attending health facilities? School-going adolescents?

These are not interchangeable populations.

Step 3 — Where?

The entire district, selected sub-counties, schools, facilities or communities?

Step 4 — When?

Current prevalence? Annual occurrence? Change from 2024 to 2026? A five-year trend?

Step 5 — Compared with what?

Previous years? Another district? Different sub-counties? Different school-attendance groups?

A more useful question

How did a clearly defined adolescent-pregnancy measure change across sub-counties in District X from 2024 to 2026?

The denominator and data source still need scrutiny, but the question is now measurable.

Your task

Rewrite this statement as an epidemiological question:

“Hypertension is very common among our patients.”

Specify what, who, where, when and the comparison or denominator you need.

WORKED EXAMPLE · Reported increase or real increase?

Return to the fictional malaria scenario.

Month Confirmed cases People tested Reporting facilities
Month 1 120 600 18
Month 2 240 1,600 30

The number of confirmed cases doubled.

But positivity among those tested changed from:

  • Month 1: 120 / 600 = 20%
  • Month 2: 240 / 1,600 = 15%

This still does not tell us the true incidence of malaria in the district. Testing may be selective; the tested population may differ; seasonality and facility reporting changed. But it demonstrates why a raw count can produce a misleading first impression.

What did we learn?

The defensible statement is:

Reported confirmed case counts increased, while positivity among those tested decreased; additional information is required to infer what happened to population disease occurrence.

The sentence separates what was observed from what is being inferred.

GRADUATE DEEPEN · From a topic to an estimand

At graduate level, an epidemiological question is not complete merely because it names a population, exposure and outcome. You should also be able to state what quantity you want to estimate and what comparison gives that quantity meaning.

A useful progression is:

Topic → question → target population → exposure/intervention strategy → outcome → time horizon → contrast → estimand → design → estimator.

Consider the vague topic hypertension control among adults with HIV. Several different questions can hide inside it:

  • Descriptive: What proportion of adults receiving ART have uncontrolled blood pressure in 2026?
  • Predictive: Among adults with controlled blood pressure today, who is most likely to become uncontrolled within 12 months?
  • Causal: What would the 12-month risk of uncontrolled blood pressure be if eligible adults received pharmacist-led medication review compared with usual care?
  • Implementation: Among facilities adopting pharmacist review, what proportion deliver the intervention with adequate fidelity and reach?

These questions require different designs and analyses. A prediction model can perform well without estimating a causal effect. A prevalence survey can describe a burden without identifying what would happen under an intervention.

The target population is not automatically the study sample

The target population is the population about which you want to make an inference. The study population is the population that is actually eligible and accessible through the study setting. The analytic sample is the subset with sufficient data for a particular analysis.

A common graduate-level error is to move silently from one to another. If your target is all adults with HIV in Uganda, but the study population is adults attending six urban referral clinics, the difference must be acknowledged. External validity is not a decorative paragraph at the end; it begins with how the target population was defined.

Define exposure strategies precisely

“Exposed versus unexposed” can be too vague for causal work. Ask whether the exposure corresponds to a strategy that could, at least conceptually, be compared.

For example, “high BMI versus low BMI” is a description of states. “A sustained weight-management intervention that reduces BMI by at least 5% versus usual care” is closer to an intervention strategy. The distinction matters because causal effects are easiest to interpret when the compared conditions are well defined.

Time zero must be aligned

Exposure eligibility, assignment/classification and start of outcome follow-up should align around a defensible time zero. If treated participants start follow-up on the date treatment begins but untreated participants are allowed to accumulate disease-free time before entering the comparison, the design can manufacture an advantage for treatment. This family of problems includes immortal-time and time-alignment biases.

LIPPS rule
Before choosing a model, write one sentence beginning: “I want to estimate…” If that sentence is ambiguous, no statistical method can rescue the study.

PhD bridge · Counterfactual language

Advanced causal inference expresses an individual causal effect by comparing outcomes under mutually exclusive exposure strategies, such as \(Y^{a=1}\) and \(Y^{a=0}\). We cannot observe both potential outcomes for the same person at the same time, which is the fundamental problem of causal inference. Population causal effects therefore require assumptions and designs that make groups informative about those missing counterfactual outcomes.

You do not need potential-outcome notation to practise good epidemiology. You do need its discipline: state the comparison, state the target population, state the time horizon, and state the assumptions that connect observed data to the quantity you want.

Graduate checkpoint

Take a proposed dissertation title and identify:

  1. the target population;
  2. the exposure/intervention strategies;
  3. the outcome and time horizon;
  4. whether the question is descriptive, predictive, causal or implementation-oriented;
  5. the estimand you would report;
  6. the design that could identify it;
  7. one assumption that, if false, would make the estimate misleading.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

1. Which part of the epidemiological definition is mainly concerned with person, place and time?

A. Application
B. Distribution
C. Determinants
D. Intervention

Answer: B. Distribution includes frequency and pattern, including person, place and time.

2. Why is a denominator important when comparing two populations?

A. It guarantees there is no bias.
B. It provides population context for the number of events.
C. It proves causation.
D. It eliminates confounding.

Answer: B. A denominator tells us the population in which the events occurred.

3. Which activity is most clearly analytic epidemiology?

A. Plotting cases by village.
B. Tabulating cases by age.
C. Comparing prior exposures in people with and without disease.
D. Counting weekly case reports.

Answer: C. The defining feature is an appropriate comparison used to assess an exposure-outcome relationship.

4. A surveillance programme broadens its case definition. What is the most immediate concern when interpreting the subsequent rise in cases?

A. The disease must have become more severe.
B. The apparent increase may partly reflect changed classification.
C. The denominator is no longer relevant.
D. Analytic epidemiology cannot be used.

Answer: B. A broader definition can identify more cases even without a true change in underlying occurrence.

5. Which statement best distinguishes epidemiology from clinical care?

A. Epidemiology never considers individuals.
B. Clinical care never uses population evidence.
C. Epidemiology primarily examines health collectively in defined populations.
D. Epidemiology is concerned only with infectious diseases.

Answer: C. The distinction is primarily the level of concern, not complete separation of the disciplines.

6. Which sequence best captures John Snow’s epidemiological contribution as presented in this lesson?

A. Model → p-value → publication
B. Description → hypothesis → comparison → action
C. Diagnosis → treatment → discharge
D. Randomisation → blinding → trial

Answer: B. His work illustrates movement from observed population pattern to hypothesis, comparison and public-health action.

7. Which is NOT one of the core public-health epidemiology functions introduced this week?

A. Surveillance
B. Field investigation
C. Analytic studies
D. Individual prescribing

Answer: D. Individual prescribing is primarily a clinical function.

8. A district reports 300 cases this year and 200 last year. What should be your first epidemiological reaction?

A. The disease increased by 50%.
B. An outbreak has occurred.
C. Determine whether definitions, population, detection and reporting are comparable.
D. Fit a regression model.

Answer: C. Comparability of measurement and population must be established before interpreting the change.

DEMONSTRATE · Week 1 Mastery Quiz

The Moodle mastery quiz is designed as a short, objective, scenario-based assessment.

Pilot specification

  • 15 questions sampled from a 40-item Week 1 bank;
  • 10 minutes initially, then calibrated using real student response-time data;
  • one question at a time;
  • question order and answer positions randomised where appropriate;
  • one graded attempt in Mastery mode;
  • explanatory feedback available in Practice mode; and
  • approved extra-time accommodations supported in institutional use.

The quiz tests whether you can recognise and apply epidemiological reasoning, not whether you can search for definitions quickly.

SUMMARY · The Week 1 mental model

DEFINE — What exactly counts as the event?
LOCATE — Who, where and when?
COUNT — How many?
CONTEXTUALISE — Out of how many, and under what measurement system?
COMPARE — Different from what?
EXPLAIN — What hypotheses could account for the pattern?
INTERPRET — What does the evidence support, and what does it not support?
ACT — What is the appropriate public-health response?

If you learn that habit, the statistics that follow will make substantially more sense.
