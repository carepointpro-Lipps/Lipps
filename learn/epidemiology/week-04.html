LIPPS LEARN · EPIDEMIOLOGY · WEEK 04

Descriptive Epidemiology: Person, Place and Time

Before you explain a pattern, describe it well enough to see what needs explaining.

Foundation Person · Place · Time Epi curves Hypothesis generation

This week in one sentence

Describe health events systematically by person, place and time, use appropriate denominators and displays, and turn observed patterns into testable hypotheses.

The LIPPS lens
Evidence becomes useful when you can explain not only what the analysis shows, but also what the design, measurement and comparison allow you to claim.

Learning outcomes

By the end of Week 4, you should be able to:

  1. explain what descriptive epidemiology contributes before analytic testing;
  2. distinguish the health event being counted from the characteristics used to describe it;
  3. construct the logic of a useful line list and recognise why data quality matters before plotting;
  4. select an appropriate time scale and distinguish secular, seasonal and outbreak-time patterns;
  5. interpret the basic purpose and limitations of an epidemic curve;
  6. describe disease by place using relevant geographic units, rates and spot maps;
  7. choose person characteristics and category widths that preserve important patterns;
  8. distinguish a high count from a high rate;
  9. use descriptive patterns to identify populations at risk and generate hypotheses;
  10. recognise outliers and apparent increases that may reflect reporting, denominator or measurement changes rather than true disease change.

ORIENT · “Cases doubled” — is that enough?

A district reports 80 malaria cases this week compared with 40 during the same week last year. That sounds like a doubling, but an epidemiologist should immediately ask what else changed.

  • Did the district population change?
  • Did testing volume change?
  • Did the case definition change?
  • Did a new reporting facility open?
  • Are the cases concentrated in one place, age group or time period?
  • Is the comparison week an appropriate expected baseline?

Descriptive epidemiology is not “just making tables.” It is the disciplined first attempt to understand what happened, to whom, where and when, and to decide what deserves explanation.

LEARN 1 · Start by defining what you are counting

A descriptive analysis is only as coherent as its event definition. Before counting, specify the disease, condition, injury, behaviour or other health event.

For outbreak work, a practical case definition commonly combines clinical criteria with restrictions on time, place and person. The suspected exposure should generally not be built into the case definition when that exposure will later be evaluated, because doing so can manufacture an association.

A useful first question is therefore:

If two analysts used this definition independently, would they classify the same people in the same way?

Definitions may legitimately evolve as knowledge improves, but changes must be documented because a broader or narrower definition can change the apparent pattern.

LEARN 2 · Build the line list before the story

A line list is a compact dataset in which each row represents one case or observation and each column represents a variable needed for description or investigation.

A practical outbreak line list might include:

Domain Example variables
Identifier unique case ID
Person age, sex, occupation, population group
Place village, parish, facility, residence or exposure location
Time date of onset, date reported, date admitted
Clinical symptoms, laboratory status, outcome
Investigation selected exposures, contact status, vaccination status

Before analysis, check duplicates, impossible dates, missing age or location, inconsistent categories and the relationship between onset date and report date.

A graph can make bad data look authoritative.
Inspect the line list before you admire the curve.

LEARN 3 · Time: choose the clock that matches the process

Time can be described at very different scales.

Secular trend examines long-term change over years or decades.
Seasonality examines recurring patterns within the year.
Day-of-week or time-of-day patterns can reveal scheduled occupational, behavioural or environmental exposures.
Epidemic time focuses on the short interval relevant to an outbreak.

The x-axis should therefore match the biology and operational question. Annual bins can hide a short outbreak; hourly bins can add noise to a chronic-disease trend.

Events such as a new intervention, a reporting change or a suspected exposure can be annotated on a time plot, but temporal coincidence by itself does not prove effect.

LEARN 4 · Epidemic curves are compressed epidemiological arguments

An epidemic curve plots the number of cases by time of onset, diagnosis or another clearly stated event. Its shape can suggest—but does not by itself prove—the pattern of spread.

Common patterns include:

  • a concentrated peak compatible with a point-source exposure;
  • a prolonged or irregular pattern compatible with continuous or intermittent common-source exposure;
  • successive waves compatible with propagated person-to-person transmission.

The curve can also reveal the magnitude and stage of an outbreak, possible outliers and a plausible exposure window when the incubation period is known.

Do not classify an outbreak from shape alone. Reporting delays, mixed exposures, secondary transmission and missing onset dates can distort the curve.

LEARN 5 · Outliers can be clues rather than rubbish

An observation far from the main pattern may represent:

  • a data-entry or recall error;
  • an unrelated illness;
  • an unusually long or short incubation period;
  • a secondary case;
  • exposure at a different time or place; or
  • the clue that links apparently separate groups.

The correct response is not automatically to delete it. Verify it, understand it, and document any exclusion.

This is a useful LIPPS habit:

Investigate surprising observations before deciding they are errors.

LEARN 6 · Place is whatever geography matters to transmission or risk

“Place” is broader than residence. Depending on the question, the relevant location may be a workplace, school, hospital ward, water point, market, travel destination or place of exposure.

Two main mapping approaches have different purposes:

Spot maps show where individual events occurred and are useful for small clusters or outbreaks.

Area maps compare numbers or rates across geographic units. When populations differ substantially, rates are usually more meaningful than raw counts.

A map shows spatial pattern; it does not automatically identify the mechanism producing that pattern. Population density, access to care, diagnostic capacity and reporting completeness can all create geographic differences.

LEARN 7 · Person: categories can reveal or conceal risk

Person variables can include age, sex, occupation, socioeconomic measures, behaviours, immune status and other characteristics relevant to the problem.

Age deserves particular care. A broad 0–4 year category may hide that nearly all excess disease is concentrated among infants. Category boundaries should therefore be chosen for epidemiological meaning, not merely because they are convenient.

Similarly, an apparent difference between social groups should not be interpreted as an inherent biological difference without examining exposure, access, measurement and structural context.

LEARN 8 · Counts answer burden; rates answer comparative frequency

A district with 500 cases may have a lower disease frequency than a district with 100 cases if their populations differ greatly.

Counts are useful for operational planning: beds, medicines, staff and laboratory workload.

Rates or proportions put events in relation to a population at risk and are more useful for comparing groups of different sizes.

Example:

District Cases Population Cases per 1,000
A 500 500,000 1.0
B 100 20,000 5.0

The smaller district has fewer cases but five times the rate.

LEARN 9 · Description generates hypotheses; it rarely settles them

Suppose cases cluster among children under five, peak after heavy rainfall and are concentrated around two water sources. Those observations can generate hypotheses about exposure and transmission.

A hypothesis should connect the pattern to a mechanism that can be evaluated. For example:

Households relying on selected surface-water sources experienced higher risk of acute watery diarrhoea than households using protected sources.

That hypothesis now suggests what comparison data must be collected.

Descriptive epidemiology therefore bridges observation → hypothesis, while analytic epidemiology adds a comparison capable of testing that hypothesis more rigorously.

LEARN 10 · Observed versus expected

A cluster is not automatically an outbreak. Investigators must consider whether occurrence exceeds what is expected for that population and period.

The expected level may come from:

  • prior weeks, months or years;
  • a comparable season;
  • surveillance records;
  • another population with similar characteristics; or
  • a deliberately constructed baseline.

An apparent increase may instead reflect population growth, improved diagnostics, duplicate reporting, a changed case definition, greater public attention or a more diligent reporting facility.

LIPPS question: What changed in the disease—and what changed in the system that observes the disease?

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

A fictional district receives 46 reports of acute watery diarrhoea over 10 days. Thirty-four reports come from two neighbouring villages. Onset dates show a sharp rise over three days, followed by a slower decline. Most patients are children, but one case occurred a week earlier in an adult trader who regularly visits both villages. A new private clinic also began reporting during the same month.

Work through the problem before looking for an exposure:

  1. Draft a usable working case definition.
  2. Identify the minimum variables for the line list.
  3. Decide whether onset date or report date should drive the epidemic curve and explain why.
  4. Calculate village-specific rates if population denominators are available.
  5. Identify which observations might be useful outliers.
  6. List at least three hypotheses suggested by the person-place-time pattern.
  7. Identify at least three non-disease explanations for the apparent increase that must be checked.
  8. State what comparison you would need next to test your leading hypothesis.

The point is not to guess the cause. The point is to produce a description strong enough to justify the next question.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. Why can an epidemic curve based on report date tell a different story from one based on symptom-onset date?
  2. A village has twice as many cases as another village but ten times the population. Which comparison is more informative: counts or rates, and why?
  3. What is wrong with including “drank from Water Point X” in the case definition when Water Point X is the exposure you plan to test?
  4. Give two plausible explanations for a late outlier on an epidemic curve other than data-entry error.
  5. Why might five-year age bands hide an important disease pattern among infants?
  6. Does a spatial cluster around a health facility prove that the facility is the source of infection? Explain.

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.

DEMONSTRATE · Week 4 mastery quiz

Pilot Mastery Quiz: 15 objective questions randomly drawn from a Week 4 bank of 50 items.

Initial time limit: 11 minutes.
Question style: interpretation of person-place-time displays, denominator choice, case-definition logic, epidemic-curve reasoning and hypothesis generation.
Presentation: one question per page; options shuffled where appropriate; one graded mastery attempt.
Practice mode: untimed with explanatory feedback.

Assessment principle
The quiz is designed so that a prepared learner can read, reason and answer efficiently. Timing is a learning-and-assessment parameter to be calibrated from real response-time data, not a substitute for good question design.

Source foundation and LIPPS extensions

Primary source foundation: CDC Principles of Epidemiology in Public Health Practice, Third Edition, especially its treatment of descriptive epidemiology, time, place, person, epidemic curves, spot maps, case definitions and observed-versus-expected reasoning.

LIPPS additions: the fictional district cases, the explicit data-quality workflow, modern wording around reporting-system changes, and the integrated “disease versus observation system” reasoning are instructional extensions rather than material reproduced from the source.

Reference: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Principles of Epidemiology in Public Health Practice, Third Edition: An Introduction to Applied Epidemiology and Biostatistics. October 2006; updated May 2012.
