LIPPS LEARN · EPIDEMIOLOGY II · WEEK 05

Survival and Longitudinal Epidemiology

Make time, censoring and competing events explicit parts of the estimand.

Advanced MSc / PhD Causal methods

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. state the target estimand in population, strategy, outcome, time and effect-measure terms;
  2. identify the assumptions required for the week’s method;
  3. distinguish identification from estimation;
  4. select an analysis that corresponds to the scientific question;
  5. interpret absolute and relative effects with uncertainty;
  6. diagnose at least one failure mode using subject-matter reasoning;
  7. implement or outline a reproducible analysis in R; and
  8. defend what the resulting estimate can and cannot support.

ORIENT · Time-to-event data are not ordinary binary outcomes

Two participants can both be event-free at study end but contribute very different information if one is followed for six years and another for six weeks. Survival methods use partial follow-up rather than discarding it.

LEARN 1 · Survival, hazards and cumulative incidence

The survival function \(S(t)\) is the probability of remaining event-free beyond \(t\). The hazard is an instantaneous rate among those currently event-free. Cumulative incidence describes the probability of experiencing an event by time \(t\).

These are linked but not interchangeable. Scientific communication is often clearer when a hazard ratio is accompanied by absolute survival or cumulative-incidence estimates at clinically meaningful times.

LEARN 2 · Censoring assumptions

Right censoring is not automatically harmless. Standard survival estimators require censoring to be independent of future outcome under appropriate conditioning. Administrative end of follow-up is often easier to defend than dropout due to worsening illness.

Inverse-probability-of-censoring weights can address informative loss under measured predictors, but extreme weights reveal weak support.

LEARN 3 · Cox model and proportional hazards

The Cox model estimates hazard ratios without specifying the baseline hazard. Check proportional hazards using graphical and residual-based diagnostics. If the effect varies over time, report time-specific effects or use a model that represents non-proportionality.

A statistically significant proportional-hazards test in a very large sample does not automatically make the model useless; judge the magnitude and substantive importance of non-proportionality.

LEARN 4 · Competing risks

When competing events prevent the event of interest, choose between cause-specific hazard questions and real-world cumulative-incidence questions. The Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard is one modelling approach, but its coefficient should not be casually interpreted as a causal risk ratio.

Often the most interpretable outputs are standardised cumulative incidences under clearly defined strategies.

LEARN 5 · Recurrent events and multi-state processes

Hospital admissions, malaria episodes and exacerbations can recur. First-event analyses discard later events. Depending on the question, consider event-rate models, Andersen-Gill approaches, gap-time models, frailty models or multi-state models.

Multi-state thinking is useful even without complex software: patients may transition from diagnosis → treatment → suppression → loss → re-engagement → death. The process itself may be the epidemiological object.

LEARN 6 · Time-dependent covariates

CD4 count, viral load, medication adherence and employment can change during follow-up. A time-updated Cox model can incorporate changing predictors, but if a time-varying confounder is itself affected by prior treatment, conventional adjustment can block part of the treatment effect and induce bias. That problem motivates Week 8.

METHODS WORKSHOP · Move from a survival curve to a defensible estimand

Suppose an oncology cohort compares treatment A with treatment B for 3-year mortality.

Kaplan-Meier first

Plot unadjusted survival and numbers at risk. This reveals follow-up support and whether one group has little information late in time.

library(survival)
km <- survfit(Surv(time, death) ~ treatment, data=d)
plot(km)

Do not report survival curves without numbers at risk; tails based on very few participants are unstable.

Cox model second

Fit the model only after defining covariates and time scale.

cox <- coxph(Surv(time, death) ~ treatment + age + stage, data=d)
cox.zph(cox)

The time scale itself matters. Age-as-time-scale can control age finely in some chronic-disease cohorts, while time-since-entry may better match the clinical process in others.

Absolute survival contrasts

Use the fitted model to estimate standardised survival curves under each treatment and report survival probabilities at 1 and 3 years. This often communicates treatment impact more clearly than a hazard ratio alone.

Left truncation

Participants who enter a cohort after becoming at risk contribute only after entry. If the analysis ignores delayed entry, survivors who live long enough to enrol can create length or survivor bias. Survival objects can represent start-stop intervals.

Time-dependent exposure

For medication initiation during follow-up, split person-time at treatment changes. Never assign the eventual treatment status backward to baseline.

# conceptual start-stop structure
Surv(tstart, tstop, event)

Competing risk worked example

If recurrence is the event and death precludes recurrence, report cumulative incidence of recurrence in the presence of death. A cause-specific Cox model addresses etiologic rate questions; a cumulative-incidence function addresses real-world probability. Choose based on the scientific objective.

Recurrent event strategies

For hospital readmission: - time-to-first-event ignores later admissions; - Andersen-Gill treats recurrent events on a common time scale; - Prentice-Williams-Peterson models ordered events; - frailty models introduce latent heterogeneity.

No method is universally best. Ask whether the policy question concerns any admission, total burden, gap time or transition process.

Multi-state models

Represent states such as “in care,” “lost,” “re-engaged,” and “dead.” Estimate transition hazards or probabilities. This is useful when programme pathways matter more than a single endpoint.

Doctoral extension · Causal survival

Causal survival analysis requires careful handling of competing events, censoring and treatment changes. Marginal structural survival models and g-formula approaches can estimate standardised survival under interventions, but interpretation depends on whether competing events are treated as part of the outcome process or hypothetical censoring events.

SEMINAR NOTES · Time scales, hazards and clinically meaningful contrasts

Choosing the time scale

Time since treatment initiation, age and calendar time each answer different questions. Age can be used as the underlying time scale when age is the dominant risk process, with delayed entry at age of cohort enrolment. Calendar time may matter during epidemics or policy changes.

Non-proportional hazards

If survival curves cross, one hazard ratio can obscure early harm and later benefit. Options include time-varying coefficients, restricted mean survival time (RMST) differences and time-specific survival contrasts.

RMST up to time \(\tau\) is the area under the survival curve:

\[RMST(\tau)=\int_0^\tau S(t)dt.\]

The difference in RMST has a direct interpretation as average event-free time gained within the horizon.

Informative entry

Left truncation assumes individuals are observed only after an entry time and survived event-free until entry. Failing to account for delayed entry can produce survivor bias in occupational and chronic-disease cohorts.

Competing-event estimands

If death competes with kidney failure, an intervention that reduces death may paradoxically increase observed kidney failure because more people survive long enough to experience it. This is not necessarily harm. Separate etiologic effects on cause-specific hazards from policy effects on cumulative incidence.

Administrative versus informative censoring

Study end is often plausibly independent of prognosis conditional on calendar time. Loss because a patient becomes too sick to attend clinic is not. Distinguish reasons for censoring and model them when possible.

Recurrent events and terminal events

For repeated hospitalisations followed by death, treating death as ordinary censoring can distort recurrent-event estimates. Joint frailty or multi-state models can account for dependence between recurrent events and terminal death.

Dynamic prediction versus causal effect

Survival models are also used for prognosis. A strong prognostic Cox model may include post-treatment biomarkers and be inappropriate for estimating a treatment effect. Again, prediction and causal inference have different variable-selection rules.

ADVANCED SYNTHESIS · Time-to-event data require a risk-set way of thinking

Survival analysis is not simply “regression with time.” The fundamental object is the evolving risk set: at each event time, who was still under observation and capable of experiencing the event?

Survival, hazard and cumulative hazard

The survival function is

\[S(t)=P(T>t).\]

The hazard \(h(t)\) is the instantaneous event rate among those event-free immediately before \(t\). The cumulative hazard \(H(t)\) integrates hazard over time, and under standard conditions:

\[S(t)=\exp[-H(t)].\]

Hazards and risks answer different questions. A hazard ratio is a ratio of instantaneous rates conditional on survival to that time; it is not generally a ratio of cumulative risks.

Kaplan-Meier estimator

The Kaplan-Meier estimator updates survival at each event time:

\[\hat S(t)=\prod_{t_j\le t}\left(1-\frac{d_j}{n_j}\right),\]

where \(d_j\) events occur among \(n_j\) people at risk. Censoring removes people from later risk sets. The estimator relies on censoring being conditionally independent of the future event process, given what has been accounted for.

Informative censoring

Loss to follow-up is not harmless merely because the proportion is small. If people with worsening disease preferentially leave care, censoring may be informative. Compare baseline and time-updated characteristics of censored versus retained participants; use inverse-probability-of-censoring weights when justified.

Cox proportional hazards

Cox regression leaves baseline hazard unspecified while assuming covariate effects multiply the hazard proportionally. Assess proportional hazards using time interactions, Schoenfeld residuals and substantive knowledge. If proportional hazards fails, consider time-varying coefficients, stratified Cox models or alternative estimands.

Absolute survival contrasts

Even when Cox regression is used, present standardised survival curves or risks at clinically meaningful times. A hazard ratio alone can conceal how much absolute event probability differs.

Restricted mean survival time

The restricted mean survival time (RMST) up to \(\tau\) is the expected event-free time through \(\tau\):

\[RMST(\tau)=\int_0^\tau S(t)dt.\]

The difference in RMST provides an absolute time-scale contrast and does not require proportional hazards.

Competing risks

If death prevents subsequent occurrence of the non-fatal outcome, censoring death as though it were ordinary loss to follow-up changes interpretation. The cause-specific hazard addresses the instantaneous event process among those currently event-free; the cumulative incidence function estimates the probability of the event in the presence of competing events. Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards answer yet another question. Match method to estimand.

Recurrent events

Repeated malaria episodes, hospitalisations or exacerbations violate a single-failure framework. Options include event-rate models, Andersen-Gill models, gap-time models and frailty approaches. Define whether the scientific target is total event burden, first event, recurrent intensity or subject-specific heterogeneity.

Time-varying covariates

Biomarkers, adherence and treatment can change during follow-up. Update covariate histories using start-stop data structures. Beware adjusting conventionally for time-varying variables that are both confounders and consequences of earlier treatment; that problem motivates marginal structural models.

Immortal-time bias

If exposure is defined by surviving long enough to receive treatment, the pre-treatment period can be incorrectly credited to the exposed group. Align eligibility, exposure assignment and follow-up; use time-varying exposure or target-trial designs when necessary.

MSc standard

Produce a Kaplan-Meier curve, estimate survival at prespecified times, fit/diagnose a Cox model, and report at least one absolute measure. Explain censoring assumptions.

PhD standard

Defend the causal meaning of the survival estimand, handle competing events and informative censoring explicitly, consider RMST and time-varying effects, and distinguish statistical proportionality from substantive causal assumptions.

DEMONSTRATE · Graduate standard

Submit one short methods memo or reproducible analysis in which you name the estimand first, justify the design/estimator, report diagnostics, and state one sensitivity analysis. Marks should be lost for an unjustified causal claim even when the code is technically correct.
