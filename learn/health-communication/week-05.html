LIPPS LEARN · HEALTH COMMUNICATION · WEEK 05

Community Engagement and Participatory Communication

Communities are not channels through which programmes deliver messages. They are partners with knowledge, power, priorities and solutions.

Participation Community listening Power RCCE

This week in one sentence

Design community engagement as a two-way, accountable process that shares information, listens, adapts interventions and builds workable solutions with affected communities.

The LIPPS lens
Good health communication asks not only whether a message was delivered, but who interpreted it, under what conditions, what action became possible, and what evidence supports the claimed effect.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. distinguish information, consultation, involvement, collaboration and shared leadership;
  2. explain why community engagement is different from mobilisation alone;
  3. map stakeholders by influence, interest, trust and role;
  4. identify power and representation problems in community participation;
  5. design feedback and accountability loops;
  6. use community listening to identify questions, rumours and barriers;
  7. explain the relationship between community engagement and trust;
  8. apply RCCE principles in health emergencies and humanitarian settings;
  9. recognise safeguarding, inclusion and do-no-harm considerations.

ORIENT · The meeting had 200 people—but no engagement

A response team holds a large community meeting during a cholera outbreak. Officials speak for 90 minutes, distribute flyers and leave. Attendance is recorded as evidence of “community engagement.” No questions are documented, women speak very little, local water vendors are absent, and the team has no mechanism to report back on concerns.

Large attendance can be mobilisation. It is not automatically participation or engagement.

LEARN 1 · Engagement is relational, not event-based

Community engagement is an ongoing process of developing relationships and structures through which affected people help shape decisions and solutions. In emergencies, WHO frames communities as partners in creating responses that are acceptable and workable.

LEARN 2 · A useful participation continuum

Communication can move from inform → consult → involve → collaborate → share leadership/empower. Not every decision can be fully delegated, but programmes should be clear about what is genuinely open to influence. Pretending to consult when decisions are already fixed damages trust.

LEARN 3 · Stakeholder mapping should include power and trust

List groups who are affected, who influence decisions, who control resources, who are trusted, who may be excluded and who can block or enable implementation. A high-ranking official may have formal power but low community trust; a local midwife or market leader may have high practical influence.

LEARN 4 · Representation is never automatic

Community leaders do not necessarily represent women, adolescents, people with disabilities, displaced people, minority language groups or stigmatised populations. Ask who is missing from the room and who may not be safe speaking publicly.

LEARN 5 · Listening creates programme data

Questions, complaints, rumours and resistance can reveal operational problems. If people repeatedly say vaccination teams arrive after working hours, the response is not merely to “correct misinformation.” It may require changing service delivery.

LEARN 6 · Feedback loops must close

A functional loop is listen → document → analyse → decide → act → report back. If communities provide information but never hear what happened, engagement becomes extractive.

LEARN 7 · Trust is built through behaviour

Transparency, consistency, respectful treatment, visible response to concerns and honest acknowledgement of constraints can strengthen trust. Communication teams cannot manufacture trust with slogans when programme behaviour contradicts the message.

LEARN 8 · RCCE links communication and operational response

Risk communication and community engagement work best when connected to surveillance, case management, vaccination, WASH, logistics and other response pillars. Community insight should change operations where evidence shows a barrier.

LEARN 9 · Participation has ethical and safeguarding requirements

Engagement should avoid exposing people to retaliation, stigma or political risk. Consent, privacy, safe participation, fair compensation where appropriate and mechanisms for complaints matter, particularly in humanitarian settings.

LEARN 10 · Measure quality, not just attendance

Useful indicators may include diversity of participation, proportion of priority concerns receiving a documented response, time to close feedback loops, changes made because of community input, trust measures and whether affected groups can explain how to raise concerns.

DEEPEN · Engagement changes decisions, not only messages

Community engagement is often weakened into “sensitisation”: officials decide, then communities are informed. Genuine participation can change what problem is prioritised, how an intervention is designed, who delivers it and how implementation is corrected.

Participation has degrees

Information sharing, consultation, involvement, collaboration and community-led decision-making are not interchangeable. The appropriate level depends on the decision, urgency, legal responsibilities and available time, but teams should be honest about which level they are offering. Calling a one-way briefing “co-design” damages trust.

Representation and power

Inviting recognised leaders is useful but insufficient. Leadership structures can exclude women, adolescents, displaced people, people with disabilities, minority language groups or politically marginalised households. Engagement plans should ask: Who is affected? Who speaks? Who is missing? Who can safely disagree?

Build a feedback loop

A functioning loop has four parts: collect concerns, analyse and prioritise them, adapt the response when justified, and communicate back what changed or why it did not. Collecting complaints without closure can become extractive.

Community listening as data

Rumours, questions, hotline logs, community health-worker reports and meeting notes can reveal confusion or distrust early. They are not automatically representative survey data. Use them as signals, triangulate them and document how decisions were made.

Worked example · Cholera prevention

If residents report that chlorinated water tastes unpleasant, the response should not begin by labelling them resistant. Investigate whether dosing is correct, whether taste is expected at safe concentrations, whether alternative unsafe water sources are easier to access, and what trusted local explanations exist. The final response may involve technical correction, transparent explanation and community monitoring—not messaging alone.

Accountability and do-no-harm

Engagement creates expectations. Never promise resources you cannot deliver. Protect confidentiality when feedback could expose individuals to stigma or retaliation. In conflict settings, even selecting meeting locations or community representatives can have political consequences.

What could fool us?

High meeting attendance can coexist with low influence. Repeated consultation can create “participation fatigue” while decisions remain unchanged. Community agreement recorded in public meetings may hide private dissent. Evaluate not only how many people participated but whose input altered what.

FIELD LAB · Design a feedback loop that actually closes

Imagine a cholera response in a large settlement. Community health workers report three recurring concerns: chlorine taste, long water-point queues and confusion about where suspected cases should go.

A weak system simply records these concerns. A stronger system assigns each concern to a decision pathway:

Signal Owner Decision Feedback to community
chlorine taste WASH technical team check dosing and explain safe range report test result and any correction
long queues operations team adjust distribution schedule or add points announce changed hours/locations
referral confusion health/RCCE team simplify referral instructions publish clear route and test comprehension

The essential step is closure. Community members should be able to see whether their input changed the response. Where a request cannot be met, explain why rather than allowing silence to imply neglect.

Participation audit

At the end of an engagement cycle, ask: Which groups contributed? Which groups were missing? What decisions were genuinely open to influence? What changed because of community input? What was outside community control? Were any people exposed to stigma or retaliation by participating?

These questions turn engagement from a meeting count into an accountability process.

APPLY · Integrated public-health problem

An Ebola preparedness programme plans vaccination and infection-prevention activities in a border district. Community leaders support the programme, but market traders fear loss of business if cases are announced, young people rely heavily on social media, and some religious leaders are sceptical of vaccination. Previous emergency teams promised feedback but rarely returned.

  1. Map at least six stakeholders by influence, trust and likely role.
  2. Identify groups likely to be underrepresented if the programme consults only formal leaders.
  3. Design three listening mechanisms.
  4. Create a feedback loop showing who documents, analyses, acts and reports back.
  5. Identify one operational issue that communication alone cannot solve.
  6. Propose indicators of engagement quality.
  7. Explain how you would engage sceptical leaders without turning them into token endorsers.
  8. Identify one safeguarding risk and mitigation.

PRACTISE · Check your reasoning

  1. Why is mobilisation not the same as engagement?
  2. What is the risk of relying only on formal leaders?
  3. What makes a feedback loop “closed”?
  4. Give one example of a community concern that should change operations rather than messages.
  5. Why can consultation damage trust when decisions are already fixed?
  6. Name one indicator of engagement quality other than attendance.

Use Practice mode to work slowly and review explanations. Mastery mode should be attempted only after you can answer these kinds of questions without searching for definitions.
